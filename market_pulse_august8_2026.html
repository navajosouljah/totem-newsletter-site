Weekend Briefing • Saturday, August 8, 2026

July Payrolls Fell 23,000 - First Net Monthly Job Loss in Months, a 106,000 Miss vs. Consensus - Confirming ADP Over Claims and Removing the September Rate Hike as the Base Case. S&P 500 Closed at a Record 7,757.64. Nasdaq Rose 1.3%. Silver Surged 4% to Near $65. 10-Year Treasury Fell to 4.61%. WTI Settled at $78.18 as the Hormuz Framework Holds Unsigned. Wednesday's CPI and USDA Crop Yield Report Are the Final Macro Tests Before the September 15-16 FOMC.

Friday's BLS July jobs report resolved the week's central labor market question sharply and clearly: nonfarm payrolls declined 23,000 in July, the first outright monthly loss in months, confirming the ADP 44,000 signal and making Thursday's resilient 199,000 claims reading the outlier - not the signal. The unemployment rate fell to 4.1%, but through labor force exit as participation dropped to a five-year low of 61.4%, not through employment gains. Downward revisions subtracted 103,000 from May and June combined. Markets interpreted the print as a rate-hold signal: the S&P 500 closed at a new all-time record of 7,757.64, the Nasdaq surged 1.3%, and Treasury yields fell sharply on reduced September hike odds. Silver surged 4% on both the rate-cut narrative and unresolved Hormuz safe-haven demand. WTI settled at $78.18 - the Iran-Oman framework remains unsigned and the US-Iran sovereign toll dispute remains open. The September rate hike probability fell to approximately 40%. Wednesday's July CPI and the USDA's first official corn yield estimate - both at 8:30 AM ET August 12 - are now the last significant macro inputs before the September 15-16 FOMC.

The Quick Read

The BLS settled the week's debate and then some: Nonfarm payrolls fell 23,000 in July - the first outright monthly decline in months - against a consensus of +83,000, missing by 106,000 and decisively confirming the ADP print over Thursday's resilient claims data. The mechanism behind the simultaneous -23,000 payrolls and 4.1% unemployment rate is critical: labor force participation fell to 61.4%, a level not seen in over five years, meaning the unemployment rate declined because workers left the labor force, not because employment improved. That distinction matters for wage pressure and Fed policy. Average hourly earnings rose 3.2% year-over-year, slowing from prior months - removing one of the three dissenters' remaining arguments for a September hike. Downward revisions of 66,000 to May and 37,000 to June added 103,000 to the downward picture. The July BLS number and the prior two months' revisions describe the same economy: hiring nearly stopped in the spring and summer of 2026.

The week's five labor market data points now form a coherent picture. ISM Manufacturing at 55.6% (Monday) said the economy is expanding; JOLTS June at 7.359 million openings (Tuesday) showed the labor market in a low-hire, low-fire equilibrium; ADP July at 44,000 (Wednesday) showed new hiring nearly stopped; initial claims at 199,000 for the third consecutive week (Thursday) showed layoffs at a 57-year low; and BLS July at -23,000 (Friday) showed net employment contracted. Those five readings describe a labor market in structural stasis - not a collapse, but not growth either. The scenario that makes all five simultaneously true is an economy where employers stopped adding workers while simultaneously being unwilling to let go of the ones they have. That is a labor market where aggregate employment is declining at the margin, driven by attrition, reduced new hiring, and workforce exit rather than layoffs. The Fed built its September hike case on a labor market generating sustained upward wage pressure. That case is harder to make when payrolls are negative and participation is at a five-year low.

Markets responded in the "bad news is good news" dynamic that has characterized much of 2026: stocks rose to records. The S&P 500 closed at a new all-time record of 7,757.64, surpassing Tuesday's 7,736.52. The Nasdaq surged 1.3% to 26,690.62. The logic is mechanical: lower September hike probability reduces the discount rate applied to future earnings, and growth stocks - particularly the rate-sensitive technology names - are the biggest beneficiaries. The Dow added 151 points to 54,036.93, recovering from Thursday's 464-point decline but not approaching Wednesday's 54,349 record. The paradox of record equity prices on a negative jobs print is real, and it is the defining feature of a market that has spent much of 2026 pricing the Fed's path rather than the economy's immediate health. For the household watching their 401(k) hit a new high on the same week payrolls went negative, the disconnect is not accidental - it is structural.

The Hormuz deal remained a framework as of Friday. WTI crude settled at $78.18, up modestly from Thursday's $77.75 close, still well above Wednesday's $75 low and still well below mid-July's $93 peak. The Iran-Oman agreement in principle has not produced a signed document. The US-Iran sovereignty dispute over transit fees remains structurally unresolved - Iran says the framework "is between Iran and Oman and is not subject to US interference" while the US has rejected any arrangement granting Iran toll authority or sovereign claim over the strait. Oil at $78 reflects approximately 50/50 market odds on deal completion. A signed deal takes WTI toward $70-72 within days; a deal collapse takes it back above $82-83.

Wednesday, August 12 is the next major data event: the BLS releases July CPI at 8:30 AM ET, and the USDA releases its first official corn yield estimate for the 2026 crop year at the same time. The double-header at 8:30 AM on Wednesday is the last major macro input before the September 15-16 FOMC meeting. If CPI comes in above consensus, the Fed's three dissenters partially restore their hike case even as the labor market weakens. If the USDA's official yield is below the 181 bushels-per-acre baseline - incorporating four consecutive weeks of declining crop ratings and July heat stress - food inflation re-accelerates. The scenario the September hold case most needs to avoid is above-consensus CPI combined with a below-trend crop yield. The September hike probability sits at approximately 40% heading into that Wednesday print. Inflation is the remaining variable that could move it back above 50%.

The mechanism is the discount rate - lower September hike odds compress the rate used to discount future earnings, and every stock becomes mathematically more valuable. Growth and technology names led; cyclicals lagged.

Bottom Line
  • The S&P 500 at 7,757.64 - a new all-time record on a day when the labor market reported its first monthly job loss in months - is the defining paradox of this market. The mechanism is straightforward: lower rate expectations increase the present value of future earnings. But the human reality beneath the record is also straightforward: 23,000 fewer people were employed in July than in June, 103,000 more than the initial readings suggested were not employed in May and June, and labor force participation is at a five-year low. Stock prices and employment levels are answering different questions. The S&P 500 is answering: "What does a September rate hold do to discount rates?" Employment data is answering: "Is the labor market growing?" On Friday, both questions were answered clearly. It is possible for both answers to be accurate simultaneously.
  • Next week's equity catalyst is Wednesday's July CPI. A below-consensus CPI reading - say, core CPI at or below 0.2% month-over-month - extends the rate-hold narrative and likely pushes the S&P 500 further above 7,757. An above-consensus print - core CPI at 0.4%+ month-over-month - partially restores the September hike thesis and tests whether the equity market can hold its record levels when the rate calculation reverses. The S&P 500's week-long rally has been built almost entirely on a single premise: the Fed will not hike in September. Wednesday's CPI is the first test of that premise's durability.

Bottom Line
  • The rate market's Friday reaction - 2-year at 4.16%, September probability at ~40% - is the bond market saying: "a labor market that lost 23,000 jobs in July, revised away 103,000 from the prior two months, and is running a five-year low labor force participation rate is not a labor market the Fed needs to hike to cool." That is a defensible position. The counterargument is that the three FOMC dissenters who voted for a hike at the July 29 meeting will not change their votes based on labor market data alone - they are running an inflation model, not a payrolls model. Their condition for supporting a September hold is not "payrolls are negative" but rather "core inflation has fallen below 3.0%." Core PCE is currently at 3.3% YoY. For the September hike to be removed from the table entirely - not just as a base case but as a live option - Wednesday's CPI would need to show a meaningful deceleration in core prices. The labor data alone got September to 40% probability. The inflation data will determine whether it goes to 20% or back to 55%.
  • The Freddie Mac 30-year at 6.69% is now the lagging indicator. The underlying market has moved lower - 10-year Treasury at 4.61%, down from 4.67% on Thursday - and next week's Freddie Mac survey should capture that improvement. The path toward 6.50% or below requires not just Friday's BLS-driven yield decline but also a confirming CPI. A 30-year mortgage at 6.50% versus 6.69% represents approximately $40 per month on a $400,000 mortgage - real money for a buyer, and the difference between a deal penciling and not for a first-time buyer already operating at affordability limits. The window between "rates fall on BLS miss" and "rates rise on hot CPI" opens and closes by Wednesday morning.

Bottom Line
  • Silver's ~4% Friday surge is the week's most instructive single commodity move. The metal sits at the intersection of three of this week's biggest stories: rate expectations (silver is a non-yielding asset that benefits from lower discount rates), geopolitical risk (Hormuz unresolved = safe-haven bid intact), and industrial demand (the jobs miss raises growth questions). On any normal week, at least one of those three would be pulling in the opposite direction from the others. This week, all three pulled toward silver simultaneously - lower rates are positive, unresolved Hormuz is positive, and even the jobs miss is arguably positive (lower rates outweigh growth concerns for silver's pricing). The result is a metal that climbed from approximately $61 last Thursday to near $65 Friday - an 8-9% gain over six trading days - without a single categorical bearish signal. The scenario that would reverse this is a hot Wednesday CPI combined with a Hormuz deal signing: rates rise (negative) and geopolitical risk premium exits (negative). That is the only scenario where silver's week unwinds.

That pattern appears when the market simultaneously prices "lower rates" (gold benefit) and "slower growth" (copper concern). A positive payrolls print would have been modestly negative for gold but strongly positive for copper - the inverse of what happened. The divergence between gold and copper is the market's explicit statement that Friday's jobs miss was read as a growth concern, not just a rate-path event. That reading is consequential for what happens to commodities next week if CPI comes in hot - a hot CPI is a rate hike signal that lifts yields (gold negative) without necessarily resolving the growth concern (copper also negative). Hot CPI would be the commodity market's worst outcome from Wednesday's print.

Real Estate

Friday's rate decline - 10-year to 4.61%, September hike odds at 40% - is the direction the housing market needed. The Freddie Mac 30-year at 6.69% is still Thursday's survey reading; next Thursday's PMMS will capture this week's yield moves and should show improvement. The question is whether the BLS miss and Wednesday's CPI together produce the sustained rate decline that moves affordability meaningfully.

30-yr fixed mortgage (Freddie Mac, August 7): 6.69% unchanged from Thursday's weekly survey release; reflects the lending environment through approximately Wednesday-Thursday of last week; with the 10-year at 4.61% Friday (versus 4.668% at the survey period), next Thursday's PMMS reading should show 6.60-6.65%; a cold Wednesday CPI that pushes the 10-year toward 4.50% could produce a Freddie Mac reading below 6.55% by August 14
15-yr fixed mortgage (Freddie Mac, August 7): 6.01% should improve modestly next week in line with short-to-medium-term Treasury rates
National median home price (Q2 2026, NAR): $434,900 +1.5% year-over-year; prices rose in 80% of metro areas in Q2 2026 (up from 71% in Q1); while price appreciation is slowing, the combination of 6.69% rates and a ~$435,000 median price keeps affordability historically constrained; the monthly payment on a median-priced home at current rates is approximately $2,600-2,700 (20% down, 30-year fixed)
Existing home sales (H1 2026): -4.2% 4.09 million SAAR in June, -2.4% month-over-month; the spring selling season produced completed sales that look positive year-over-year (contracts signed when rates briefly dipped), but new contract activity is weakening as rates stayed above 6.65% through summer; that leading indicator deterioration will show in completed sales data 30-60 days from now
Foreclosure starts (H1 2026, ATTOM): 164,566 +18% year-over-year; June alone +20% YoY; concentrated in markets where median home prices rose most sharply in 2021-2023; FHA delinquency at 11.88%, approximately nine percentage points above conventional delinquency; lower-income and first-time buyers under sustained affordability stress
Bottom Line
  • The housing market's most direct short-term catalyst is now Wednesday's CPI print, not the BLS report. Friday's jobs miss moved Treasury yields lower - the 10-year fell to 4.61% - and that rate decline should produce a lower Freddie Mac reading next Thursday. But the pathway from 6.69% to the 6.25-6.50% range that would meaningfully re-engage sidelined buyers requires not just a labor market miss but a confirming inflation deceleration. A buyer running the math on affordability at 6.69% versus 6.40% is looking at approximately $180 per month on a $400,000 mortgage - the difference between a deal working and not for many first-time buyers. That $180 gap does not close on Friday's payrolls print alone; it closes if Wednesday's CPI prints below consensus and the 10-year follows toward 4.45-4.50%.
  • The foreclosure data - 164,566 starts in H1 2026, up 18% year-over-year - is the K-shaped signal within housing. The national median price at $434,900 rising in 80% of metro areas is the top of the K: owners with equity, conventional loans, and stable employment are holding well. The 11.88% FHA delinquency rate and 18% foreclosure start increase is the bottom of the K: first-time buyers who stretched to enter the market at 2022-2023 prices with FHA loans at rates they are now struggling to service. The housing market is not crashing in aggregate. It is diverging in two directions simultaneously, and the divergence between NAR's national median price data and ATTOM's foreclosure data is the same divergence visible in every K-shaped reading of 2026: the aggregate statistic is fine; the distribution within it is not.

Friday's BLS print - -23,000 payrolls, 61.4% participation, 3.2% wage growth - is the household finance story of the week. The labor market is not generating the income growth that absorbs 22%+ credit card APRs, 6.69% mortgages, and a 2.7% savings rate. Gas prices moving toward $4.00 nationally provide the week's one clear household positive.

BLS July payrolls: -23,000 the household income story for August: employment contracted in July; the people who lost jobs in local government education (-50,000) and retail trade (-19,000) are not made whole by a stock market record; for the households in those specific sectors, the week's most important data point is not the S&P 500 close
Labor force participation (July): 61.4% five-year low; the people who left the labor force in July are not counted as unemployed, which is why the unemployment rate fell to 4.1% even as payrolls declined; participation at 61.4% means 38.6% of the working-age population is neither employed nor actively looking; the structural decline in participation is a long-term household income headwind that the monthly headline numbers obscure
Average hourly earnings (July): $37.62, +3.2% YoY slowing from prior months; wage growth at 3.2% is above the Fed's 2% inflation target but below the current 3.3% core PCE - meaning real wages are approximately flat; households are not falling further behind, but they are not gaining purchasing power either at the aggregate level
PCE inflation (June): -0.1% MoM, +3.7% YoY headline; core PCE +0.1% MoM, +3.3% YoY; July CPI releases Wednesday August 12; if CPI tracks PCE, the Fed is still 1.3 percentage points above its inflation target on core even with the labor market now contracting
Gas prices (national avg, Aug 8): $4.022/gallon down from $4.063 last week; the week's clearest household budget positive; approximately 50% of US states now below $4.00/gallon; the $0.076/gallon weekly decline saves the median household approximately $1.10 per fill-up; the cumulative decline from mid-July's $4.09+ peak is approximately $0.07-0.10/gallon nationally, or roughly $1.50-2.00 per fill-up
Credit card APR (composite avg): ~22.18% will not decline until the Fed cuts rates; Friday's BLS miss reduces September hike probability but the path to actual rate cuts remains 2-3+ meetings away; households carrying revolving balances continue to pay over $220 per year per $1,000 on their card; if September is a hold and the first cut comes in early 2027, credit card rates begin declining approximately 18+ months after the July 29 hold
Personal savings rate (June): 2.7% spending continues to outrun income at the national level; the combination of -23,000 July payrolls and slowing wage growth at 3.2% YoY means income growth continues to decelerate; at negative payrolls, the national savings rate is under pressure from both directions: income falls and households that lose jobs draw down savings
Bottom Line
  • The week's K-shaped household finance picture is unusually clear. At the top: S&P 500 at an all-time record, IBIT Bitcoin ETF inflows of $86M on Friday, gold near $4,300, silver at $65. At the bottom: -23,000 net payrolls, 61.4% labor force participation (five-year low), 22.18% credit card APR, 2.7% savings rate, FHA delinquency at 11.88%. Both sets of readings are real. They describe the same economy from two different vantage points. The household with a paid-off mortgage, a well-diversified 401(k), and stable employment looked at this week and saw a record S&P 500 and falling gas prices. The household carrying a balance on a 22% APR card, renting at elevated prices, and working in retail or local government education looked at this week and saw something different: negative job growth in their sectors, a rising foreclosure rate, and a savings rate below 3% that offers no cushion if that job loss reaches them. The Federal Reserve's September decision - which the market now estimates at 60% hold / 40% hike - will be made against both of those realities simultaneously.
  • Gas at $4.022 nationally is the week's one unambiguous household budget positive that applies across income distribution. The $0.076/gallon weekly decline, compounding the prior week's decline from $4.098, adds up to approximately $0.076 per gallon nationally since August 1. At 12 fill-ups per year for the median household, the cumulative weekly decline from mid-July's peak is approximately $15-20 per year in savings - a modest but real relief. If WTI holds near $78 and the transmission lag continues, national averages move toward $3.95-4.00 by August 15-20. For the Gulf Coast and Midwest consumer already at $3.51-3.61, the sub-$3.50 threshold is within reach. For California, still at $5.40-5.55, the refinery constraint makes $5.00 the near-term floor regardless of crude movements.

IBIT inflows of $86.71M on Friday confirmed continued institutional accumulation. XRP remains near the $1.00 support level with both of its near-term catalysts (ETF inflows, CLARITY Act) stalled.

Bitcoin (BTC): ~$65,000 Friday and Saturday morning +modest gain on Friday's dollar weakness and falling rate expectations; IBIT ETF inflows of $86.71M on Friday out of $98.85M total industry inflows; IBIT cumulative inflows at $61.17 billion; the week's BTC range ($63,000-$65,000) illustrates the uncorrelation thesis - a week that included a 28.7% individual stock crash (AppLovin), a negative payrolls print, and a semiconductor market rout produced a 3-4% range in Bitcoin rather than the correlated equity move that short-term traders expected
Ethereum (ETH): ~$1,900 Friday-Saturday holding the $1,807 support level; the CLARITY Act provides a constructive regulatory backdrop for ETH as well as XRP; rate-sensitive positioning modestly improved with Friday's yield decline
XRP (Ripple): ~$1.03 Friday; approximately -5.5% on the week the $1.00 psychological support level remains the key near-term floor; 52-week range $1.01-$3.38; both near-term catalysts have now stalled simultaneously - spot XRP ETF inflows collapsed to $27.29 million for all of July (from $666 million in the November 2025 launch month) and the CLARITY Act was sidelined when the Senate went on recess without a vote; the Senate returns September 14 - the same week as the September 15-16 FOMC meeting; a death cross remains on the XRP chart; ETF cumulative inflows at $1.44 billion since launch (positive but decelerating sharply); classified as a Digital Commodity by SEC and CFTC in March 2026 following the August 2025 settlement; on-chain metrics remain weak - Daily Active Addresses at 25,350, near the second-lowest reading of 2026; Ripple's August 1 escrow unlock released 1 billion XRP with ~300 million net new supply
Rate-path context for crypto: September hike probability at approximately 40% after Friday's BLS; a September hold is now the base case; the path from "hold" to "cut" is still uncertain and depends on Wednesday's CPI; crypto does not need a rate cut to perform well - it needs the hike to be off the table; Friday's move in that direction is crypto-positive at the margin, though the structural Bitcoin bid from institutional ETF flows is larger than any single rate-path signal
CLARITY Act status: Senate went on recess without a floor vote; returns September 14; passage odds fell to approximately 30% for 2026 after the recess sidelining; the September 14 return opens a new vote window but legislative calendar priority will compete with other fall agenda items
Bottom Line
  • Bitcoin's behavior this week - a 3-4% range during one of the most volatile macro weeks of 2026 - is the strongest single-week evidence for the uncorrelation thesis that institutional buyers have been citing. A week that included a historic semiconductor sell-off, an ADP miss of 44,000, a negative BLS print of -23,000, and a 464-point Dow decline produced Bitcoin trading in a $63,000-$65,000 range with consistent institutional ETF inflows on every dip. BlackRock's IBIT took in $86.71M on Friday - the day the labor market reported its worst month in years - suggesting that the institutional buyers treating Bitcoin as a portfolio diversifier are not reacting to short-term macro volatility. That is what uncorrelated assets do in practice: they do not move with the catalyst you expect them to, and they attract buying when other assets are volatile. Whether Bitcoin can maintain this uncorrelation through a genuinely severe economic deterioration - not a soft-landing wobble but a recession - remains untested. This week was not that test. But it was the closest thing to one that 2026 has produced, and Bitcoin passed it.
  • XRP at $1.03 is the clearest illustration of what happens to a crypto asset when its two primary catalysts both stall simultaneously. The CLARITY Act sidelining and the ETF inflow collapse to $27M in July (from $666M at launch) are not independent - they are causally related. Institutional buyers who were waiting for legislative clarity before making material XRP allocations are still waiting. The ETF infrastructure exists, the regulatory framework (Digital Commodity designation) is more favorable than it was a year ago, and the Ripple settlement is complete. But "infrastructure without flows and legislation without passage" describes the current state exactly. The Senate returns September 14. If the CLARITY Act reaches a floor vote in the September-October window, the ETF flow/CLARITY Act catalyst story becomes live again. Until then, the $1.00 support level is the number to watch.

Brazil: Copom fourth consecutive rate cut; Selic rate at 14.00%; Brazil's cycle is running 12-18 months ahead of US rate policy; with the US September hike now at 40% probability and the first US cut potentially moving earlier into the discussion, Brazil's current cycle is the most relevant global analog for what a US pivot looks like in practice

Bottom Line
  • The Korean semiconductor stabilization after Wednesday's -10% SK Hynix session is the international data point that matters most for the AI investment thesis. Goldman Sachs and JPMorgan maintaining Buy ratings at 3.5-3.6x forward P/E is institutional money saying explicitly: this is a valuation opportunity within a structural trend, not the end of the trend. The AI infrastructure buildout - Nvidia, AMD, hyperscaler capex - is not ending because SK Hynix and Samsung are in an inventory correction cycle. Memory chip cycles have always involved periods where supply builds ahead of demand, prices compress, and stock prices correct sharply. What is happening in Korea is the same cycle it always is, occurring inside a larger demand expansion for AI compute that has no precedent. The distinction between "inventory cycle within a structural expansion" and "structural demand collapse" is the most consequential question in technology investing right now, and the major investment banks are voting clearly on one side of it.
  • Brazil's Selic rate at 14.00% after four consecutive cuts provides a useful reference point for what the US rate cycle looks like 12-18 months ahead. Brazil began cutting in the first half of 2025, when its own labor market and inflation profile aligned with the conditions now developing in the US - a softening labor market, inflation above target but decelerating, and an economy that had absorbed a significant rate hiking cycle without catastrophic damage. The US is not Brazil - its economy is larger, its inflation persistence is different, and its fiscal situation is materially different. But the sequencing of a rate cycle (hike - hold - cut) and the conditions that trigger each phase are universal. If the US September decision is a hold and the first cut comes in Q1 2027, the US is following a timeline roughly consistent with Brazil's. The 14.00% Brazilian Selic is not a destination for US rates; it is a reference point for how rate cycles resolve when central banks have done enough tightening and the labor market begins to soften.
