The Scarcity Illusion: Dysprosium Up 242%, the Rare Earth ETF Down 31%

Every issue, we analyze the published strategies of prominent voices in the wealth-building space. Where we agree, we say so. This week we take on the loudest minerals trade on the feed: rare earth miners, sold on the promise that a shortage of the metal means a payday for the shares. The one-word challenge is scarcity.

This Week's Pitch

Rare earths are a group of 17 metals. A few of them, like neodymium, praseodymium, dysprosium, and terbium, go into the permanent magnets inside electric motors, wind turbines, data center equipment, and defense systems. The problem buyers are trying to solve is simple: they need these metals, and one country controls most of the supply.

The numbers behind that worry are real. S&P Global, citing the International Energy Agency, reports that China accounted for 61% of global mined rare earth supply and 91% of global refining and processing capacity for the major rare earths in 2024. On November 9, 2026, China's Ministry of Commerce extended the suspension of some export controls to January 10, 2027. Controls on seven other rare earths, including dysprosium and terbium, stayed in force.

The pitch that follows is everywhere. On November 5, 2026, a Motley Fool column on MP Materials and USA Rare Earth said China's export controls "are fundamentally bullish for domestically based rare-earth miners in the long term." In May, a MarketBeat piece ran under the headline "3 Rare Earth Stocks That Win No Matter What China Does Next."

The challenged piece: the rare earth miner pitch, which says that because the metal is scarce and Washington is backing domestic supply, the stocks and the funds that hold them are a strategic place for your money.

The Scarcity Playbook

Strip away the headlines and most versions run the same five steps:

  1. Lead with the chokepoint. China mines most of the supply and refines almost all of it. That part is true.
  2. Point to the price of the metal. Western oxide prices are up sharply this year, and the charts look like a straight line.
  3. Add Washington. Price floors, offtake deals, CHIPS Act money, and strategic stockpiles. The government is on your side.
  4. Hand you a ticker. A miner, a magnet maker, or a fund that holds dozens of them, like REMX.
  5. Call the drop an entry point. When the shares fall, the story says the thesis is intact and the price is a gift.

What the Numbers Show Today

The metal side. In its November 9, 2026 report, S&P Global said Platts assessed dysprosium oxide, delivered to North America, at $4,100 per kilogram on October 30, up from a launch price of $1,200 on March 31. Terbium oxide went from $4,900 to $8,100 per kilogram over the same months. Samarium oxide went from $175 to $1,200. If you were a buyer of these metals outside China, scarcity hit you hard this year.

The share side. VanEck's own page for its Rare Earth and Strategic Metals ETF (REMX) showed a net asset value of $51.19 and a year-to-date NAV return of -30.55% as of November 12, 2026. The fund holds 37 positions and $1.43 billion in net assets, and lists "Volatile Industry Dynamics" among its own reasons to know the fund. REMX closed at $50.88 on November 12, down 4.23% that day, according to StockAnalysis. StockScan puts its 52-week high at $111.55 on May 7, 2026.

The two names in the Motley Fool column tell the same story. On The Motley Fool's quote data as of 4:00 PM ET on November 12, MP Materials closed at $37.45 against a 52-week range of $31.90 to $76.80. USA Rare Earth closed at $8.07 against a 52-week range of $6.84 to $32.80.

Three Leaks Behind the Word

Leak 1 - The holder of the shares is not the holder of the metal. When you buy a rare earth stock, you do not own dysprosium. You own a slice of a company that has to dig, separate, refine, permit, finance, and sell it, often years before the first ton ships. Higher metal prices help some of those companies. They do not pay the shareholder directly, and they do not stop the share price from falling on the next headline. This year proved it: the oxide price more than tripled, and the fund that holds the miners lost about a third.

Leak 2 - The same policy that creates the shortage can end it overnight. The shortage outside China exists because Beijing restricts exports. That means the trade rises and falls on decisions made in a ministry in Beijing and in the White House, on dates nobody in the trade controls. The current pause runs to January 10, 2027. A pause can be extended, shortened, or reversed. Price floors and offtake deals from Washington help a few named companies. They are contracts with those companies, not with you.

Leak 3 - The fund spreads the risk around, but it is still the same risk. REMX is diversified across 37 holdings, and that is a real feature. But they all ride the same policy cycle and the same commodity cycle. On VanEck's country breakdown as of October 31, 2026, 29.19% of the fund's net assets sat in Chinese companies. In a trade sold as a way to get away from China, nearly a third of the fund is in China.

Where We Second Them

The chokepoint is real. An estimated 91% of refining capacity in one country is a genuine supply risk for every buyer of magnets. Nobody is making that up, and we do not pretend otherwise.

The metal prices are real. Dysprosium oxide at $4,100 per kilogram in North America, up 242% since March, is a published Platts assessment, not a forecast.

The government support is real. The Motley Fool column describes a 10-year Department of Defense price floor of $110 per kilogram for MP Materials' NdPr products and up to $1.6 billion in CHIPS Act funding for USA Rare Earth. Those are real commitments to real companies.

You can get out any trading day. REMX trades on an exchange, publishes its holdings daily, and charges a 0.53% expense ratio. On access to your money, the fund beats us outright, and our scorecard says so. In a long policy-driven shortage that lifts the miners' earnings and sentiment together, this is the regime where the trade wins.

The Scarcity Question

Now run the money. Put $100,000 into REMX at the start of 2026. On VanEck's NAV return of -30.55% through November 12, that is about $69,450 today, before any taxes, and the shortage everyone was pointing at actually happened. That is arithmetic from the fund's own published number, not a prediction of ours. Anyone who bought near the May high is further behind.

Here is the problem in one sentence: the pitch sells you the buyer's problem, a shortage of metal, and hands you a product, a share price, that does not solve it. Being right about scarcity did not protect anyone's savings this year.

Final Thought

The rare earth shortage is not imaginary. China held an estimated 91% of rare earth refining capacity in 2024, it still restricts seven of these metals, and buyers in North America paid $4,100 a kilogram for dysprosium oxide at the end of October. The industry's word is "scarcity." For the people who bought the shares this year, scarcity showed up in the metal and a 30.55% loss showed up in the fund.

The shortage is a problem for the companies that need magnets. A share price does not solve it for you. We fund what has already been sold. And we protect the capital behind it.
