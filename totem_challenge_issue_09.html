The Volatility Toll: Darius Dale's Melting Dollar Playbook

Darius Dale and Anthony Pompliano make the sharpest case yet that the dollar's purchasing power is in structural decline. We second the diagnosis almost line by line. We challenge the prescription: that the way out is to ride volatile assets and forecast your way to safety.

14 min read Share

Every issue, we analyze the published strategies of prominent voices in the wealth-building space. Where we agree, we say so. Where we differ, we run both strategies through our six-question Wealth Preservation Blueprint, send the comparison to the creators directly, and publish their response - or the lack of one. This week we are turning to macro strategist Darius Dale of 42 Macro and his July conversation with Anthony Pompliano on why the dollar keeps buying less, who that hurts most, and what he says an investor should do about it.

This Week's Experts

Darius Dale is the founder and CEO of 42 Macro, a research firm that packages institutional-grade quantitative risk management for individual investors. He spent over a decade as a managing director at Hedgeye Risk Management, advising some of the largest fixed income and multi-asset managers in the world. Anthony Pompliano is an investor and the host of The Pomp Podcast, and a returning voice in these pages - we covered his real economy thesis in Issue 06.

The challenged piece: their July 2026 conversation on The Pomp Podcast, recorded ahead of the Fed's July meeting, covering the Cantillon effect, the K-shaped economy, and the Fed's new posture under Chair Kevin Warsh.

The 42 Macro Frame

Dale's argument builds in a specific sequence, and it deserves to be laid out on its own terms:

  1. Invert the prices. Stop asking what groceries cost in dollars. Ask what one dollar buys - of the S&P, of gas, of food. Inverted, the chart shows a multi-decade structural decline in purchasing power.
  2. Name the mechanism. Newly created money reaches asset markets first. Asset owners' purchasing power compounds while median wages crawl. Economists call it the Cantillon effect.
  3. Map the K. Monetary, fiscal, and regulatory policy have each channeled gains toward asset owners for decades, producing what Dale calls a K-shaped economy: one branch rising, one flat.
  4. Respect the regime change. Chair Warsh is stepping back from forward guidance, letting markets price the neutral rate in real time. Dale argues this deliberately injects volatility into the short end of the rate curve.
  5. Watch capital scarcity. Global savings growth is slowing while sovereign deficits and AI capital spending compete for the same pool. Rising market-implied neutral rates carry the message.
  6. Stay on the right side of market risk. Own assets, and manage the exposure with quantitative systems built to maximize upside capture in bull markets and minimize downside capture in bear markets.

Inside the Frame

A dollar that buys less every year. Pompliano opens with the arithmetic: by his count, inflation has compounded at roughly 4% per year since 2019, and government data puts grocery prices up roughly 29% since early 2020. Dale's response is the sharpest line in the episode: we did not forget how to make food. There is no shortage of arable land or crop science. If groceries cost more, the unit of measurement is what changed.

The Cantillon effect, spelled out. Dale's core mechanism is the gap between two growth rates. When asset prices rise 20% in a year and a median paycheck rises 4% to 5%, the asset owner's claim on goods and services compounds away from the wage earner's. The gap widens quietly, year after year, until the two groups are shopping for the same house or the same car with very different amounts of purchasing power. His conclusion: inflation lands hardest on the people least equipped to absorb it.

The K-shaped policy stack. Dale traces the divergence to policy, not accident. Monetary: the Fed's roughly $7 trillion balance sheet and a policy stance he measures as persistently easier than a baseline Taylor rule. Fiscal: by his math, federal spending that reaches asset-heavy sectors through defense contracts and interest payments runs about double the roughly $1.2 to $1.3 trillion in means-tested programs. Regulatory: the long migration from defined benefit pensions to defined contribution plans, offshoring, and consolidation. Each channel, he argues, feeds the same divergence.

A new kind of Fed. Dale's read on Chair Warsh is notable because it cuts against his own industry's convenience. Fixed income managers, he says, are annoyed: the era of easy money made by gaming the Fed's forward guidance is ending. But he argues the change is healthy. Less over-communication means markets price the neutral rate in real time, which compresses the range of extreme outcomes in both directions. He also flags Warsh's view of the AI boom as productivity-enhancing and structurally disinflationary, while crediting him for refusing to set policy on a five-year technology forecast.

Capital getting scarce at the margins. Dale's most striking data point: trailing ten-year growth in global savings is running near 55%, against a long-run mean near 90%, at the same time that sovereign deficits (the US near 6% of GDP against a 3% long-run mean, by his figures) and an AI capital spending boom are demanding more capital than ever. His market-implied estimate of the neutral rate has risen by roughly two rate hikes in a matter of months. The market, in his telling, is telling the Treasury to pay up.

Dale closes by pointing to Peter Turchin's wealth pump research, a multi-decade academic database of societies with similar internal divergences, as the reason he treats the K-shaped economy as a structural problem worth solving rather than a talking point. We will leave the historical modeling to the historians. The economic mechanism stands on its own.

Where We Second Dale and Pompliano

There is more agreement here than in any issue we have published.

The inversion is the right lens. Pricing the dollar in goods instead of goods in dollars is exactly how a capital allocator should think. The purchasing power decline is real, structural, and measured in decades, not quarters.

The K-shaped lens is our lens. Readers of the Market Pulse know we refuse to average away divergence. "Inflation is moderating" is a lazy sentence if asset owners and wage earners are living in two different economies. Dale names the mechanism precisely, and he argues it from data rather than outrage.

Idle cash is a losing position. If the measuring stick shrinks every year, capital sitting still is capital in slow retreat. We have made this exact argument since Issue 01.

Discipline beats emotion. Dale built quantitative risk systems for institutions managing trillions and now sells them to regular investors at accessible prices. Bringing institutional discipline to household capital is honorable work, and he is genuinely good at it.

Where We Challenge: The Volatility Toll

Our singular point of divergence is the prescription.

Dale's escape route from the melting dollar is to own the assets the melting inflates, and to manage the ride with forecasting. His own words describe the new Fed era: harder for investors who anchored on forward guidance, easier "for the rest of us who can forecast accurately."

That conditional clause is the toll booth. The strategy converts an inflation problem into a forecasting problem, and for most households the second problem is no easier than the first. The reader must get the regime right, the entry right, the exit right, and the position size right, cycle after cycle, against professionals doing the same thing with more information.

And even a correct forecast collects a variable payment. The market pays what the market pays, on its own schedule, with no counterparty obligated to deliver a return. Best-in-class risk systems can trim the drawdowns. They cannot insure them. A stop-loss is a tool. It is not a policy.

"You cannot insure a forecast. You can insure a purchase order."

Note what Dale himself says the Fed is now doing: deliberately injecting volatility into the rate curve so markets price reality in real time. He argues, credibly, that this is better for the economy. But for the individual riding assets as an inflation defense, it means the toll is going up.

Totem's structure sits on the other side of that toll booth. Member capital funds documented purchase orders from verified Fortune 100 buyers on a fixed 90-day cycle. The compensation is a 5% origination fee per cycle, fixed and known upfront. Both principal and the cycle fee are covered under trade credit insurance arrangements with our carrier - a century-old, A-rated global insurance group that stands behind a meaningful share of world trade. No part of the outcome depends on where the S&P closes, what R-star does, or whether anyone's macro model got the quarter right.

None of this makes asset ownership wrong, and Dale's systems exist precisely because the game is hard. Our point is narrower: a return that depends on forecast accuracy is a fundamentally different instrument from a fee fixed by contract. The first defends purchasing power if you play well. The second does not ask you to play.

Final Thought

Dale and Pompliano are right about the melting dollar, and they say it better than almost anyone. But an answer to a currency you cannot trust should not be a forecast you have to keep getting right. A fee fixed by contract, on a fixed clock, with insurance arrangements standing behind it, asks nothing of your crystal ball. Match your capital to the structure, with eyes open on the trade-offs.
