Weekly Briefing • Friday, July 31, 2026

Amazon Tops $200B in Quarterly Revenue - AWS +37% to $42.2B in Fastest Growth in 18 Quarters. AMZN +12% Premarket. Apple Falls 7% on Q4 Guidance and Services Miss. KOSPI Surges 18% in Historic Single-Day Rebound - SK Hynix +30%, Samsung +27%. Nasdaq Futures +1.2%.

The Quick Read

The Amazon result is the second AI infrastructure verdict in three days - and it lands on the same side as Microsoft. AWS growing 37% year-over-year to $42.2 billion - its fastest growth in 18 quarters - is a direct answer to the question Meta's result left open on Wednesday. Microsoft demonstrated that AI capital expenditure can produce near-term cloud revenue acceleration. Meta demonstrated the opposite: $31 billion in Q2 AI CapEx with margin collapse and an EPS miss. Amazon's result aligns with Microsoft's: revenue over $200 billion for the first time, AWS at its fastest growth in four-and-a-half years, and an EPS of $5.75 - though the EPS number requires an asterisk. Much of Amazon's earnings beat came from a $53.4 billion non-operating pre-tax gain on its Anthropic investment, not from core operations. Strip that out and the core beat is narrower. But on the metric that matters for the AI thesis - cloud growth - Amazon has now confirmed that the infrastructure buildout is generating measurable returns on the demand side.

The KOSPI's 17.91% single-session gain is the clearest market signal of the week. After three consecutive days of losses - including two circuit breaker activations and a 22% monthly decline through Thursday, the worst since 1997 - South Korea's benchmark index staged its largest single-day gain in its history on Friday morning. SK Hynix closed at the 30% daily upper limit. Samsung gained 27%. This was not a partial recovery; it was a categorical reversal. The move confirms what many chip-sector observers suspected: the three-week selloff was driven by forward fear, not backward fundamentals. Samsung had already reported record Q2 operating profit last week. SK Hynix's memory business was healthy. The market was pricing the risk that AI demand would disappoint. Amazon's AWS at 37% growth removes the most credible version of that fear.

Apple's result is more complicated. Revenue of $109.4 billion beat estimates. EPS of $2.02 beat the $1.89 estimate. iPhone grew 22% year-over-year. Mac revenue topped expectations. But Services - Apple's highest-margin segment - came in at $30.74 billion, missing the $31.22 billion estimate. More consequentially, Q4 guidance of 9-11% growth fell short of the 12% consensus, and management cited DRAM and NAND price inflation as an accelerating headwind. CEO Tim Cook noted that memory costs are expected to continue rising through September. The -7% premarket move is not about Q3 - it is about the quarter ahead and the margin trajectory. Apple's business is not broken; but the expectation embedded in its valuation was for accelerating Services growth and margin expansion. Both are under pressure simultaneously.

The split between Amazon and Apple mirrors the split between Microsoft and Meta three days earlier. In all four cases, the companies that can demonstrate AI-driven revenue acceleration - Microsoft via Azure, Amazon via AWS - are being rewarded disproportionately. The companies where AI capital costs are rising faster than AI-attributable revenue - Meta, and now arguably Apple facing higher memory costs without a corresponding AI revenue line - are being penalized. The market is building a two-tier AI earnings framework in real time. The first tier: cloud platforms where AI demand pulls through into measurable growth. The second tier: consumer hardware and advertising platforms that are paying for AI infrastructure without a clear quarterly return. That framework is now three weeks old and has been tested by four major earnings reports. It has held each time.

The end of July deserves its own note. The S&P 500 finished the month slightly negative at approximately -0.9% despite closing Thursday at 7,437.64 - a +1.7% session. The month contained a chip-rout that erased roughly 22% from the KOSPI, a hawkish FOMC with three active dissents for a hike, a US-Iran conflict that pushed Brent above $90, an EPS miss from Meta, a guidance miss from Apple, and a June PCE print that - at -0.1% MoM - was the first monthly headline price decline in six years. That July ends near flat for the S&P 500 reflects the strength of the Microsoft and Amazon earnings, which arrived at the end of the month and offset most of the prior three weeks of damage. The question for August is whether the recovery that began Thursday carries through, or whether the October September-hike probability at 60%, oil above $90, and the KOSPI's still-fragile recovery - despite today's historic gain - reassert themselves.

Amazon's cloud platform is directly monetizing AI inference and training demand. Apple's hardware supply chain is directly paying higher DRAM and NAND prices because of the same AI demand. The irony is symmetrical: AI demand is simultaneously the best growth driver for cloud platforms and an emerging cost headwind for consumer hardware manufacturers who depend on the same memory components that AI accelerators require.

The S&P's relative resilience reflects the concentration of AI gains in US cloud stocks, which are well-represented in the index, while the AI hardware stress is more visible in Asia where the chip manufacturers are listed.

Interest Rates

The FOMC held 9-3 Wednesday with three dissenters voting for a hike. September hike probability repriced from 80% cut to approximately 60% hike in under three hours. Thursday's GDP/PCE data (Q2 +1.5%, core PCE +3.3% YoY, headline PCE -0.1% MoM) added incremental complexity - slower growth and decelerating inflation give the Fed a case for patience that the three dissenters clearly do not find persuasive. The 10-year Treasury has held near 4.677%. The July 31 session will close out a month defined by the most hawkish FOMC surprise since the hiking cycle began.

Bottom Line
  • The 10-year Treasury holding at 4.677% through Thursday's softer GDP and PCE data tells you the bond market is not yet persuaded that September will be a hold rather than a hike. Under ordinary circumstances, a GDP miss (+1.5% vs +1.8% est.) and a core PCE deceleration (+3.3% from +3.4%) would push the 10-year lower. The fact that it held steady means the bond market is treating the hawkish committee distribution - three named dissenters voting for a hike at a meeting where zero members voted for a cut - as the dominant signal, not the softer macro data. The macro data says "be patient." The committee distribution says "three of twelve members have already decided patience is over." The bond market is pricing somewhere between those two positions.
  • The 60% September hike probability will be tested by every data release between now and September 15. The next major inputs are: July jobs report August 7 (the first post-FOMC employment read), the August 4 USDA crop progress report (affects food-price expectations for the September CPI), and the August CPI itself on September 11. If the July jobs report is strong, hike probability rises further. If it softens, it creates a potential release valve. The Gordian knot is that the three FOMC dissenters voted on the basis of their read of the current data - not a hypothetical future jobs report. A single softer print is unlikely to change three already-committed dissenting votes into hold votes. The more likely path to reducing September hike probability is for both the jobs report and the August CPI to surprise to the downside.

Bottom Line
  • The CD decision calculus shifted decisively on Wednesday and has not shifted back. Before the FOMC, locking in a 1-year CD at 4.50% was described as a race against September rate cuts. Now, with September hike probability at 60%, the most likely outcome is that 4.50% rates hold through year-end or rise further. A household choosing between a 4.50% 1-year CD and a 4.15% high-yield savings account faces a structurally different question than they did two weeks ago. The savings account offers flexibility if rates continue rising; the CD locks in the current rate against the possibility that September holds steady. Neither is wrong - but the case for the CD as "locking in before cuts arrive" is no longer the correct framing. Rates may not be coming down this year.

The conventional housing-market narrative focuses on the seller-buyer standoff and inventory lock-in from low fixed-rate mortgages. The foreclosure data reveals a parallel process: distressed supply is building from the bottom of the market upward. At 6.66% mortgage rates, there are fewer qualified buyers to absorb that supply, which creates downward pressure in the lower and middle price tiers even as the overall median holds at a record. The median is not one market; it is an average of three increasingly divergent ones.

Household Finance

Headline PCE fell -0.1% MoM - the first monthly price decline in six years - driven by lower energy prices. But core PCE held at +3.3% YoY, still more than a percentage point above the Fed's 2% target. The personal savings rate at 2.7% continues to compress. Consumers are spending beyond income growth, funded in part by a credit market that is simultaneously at record stress levels.

Bottom Line
  • The personal savings rate declining from 4.5% in January to 2.7% in June is the household finance story of the first half of 2026. Consumers have continued to spend, and the aggregate spending data looks healthy in the PCE print. But the savings rate compression reveals the mechanism: spending has been sustained by drawing down savings buffers accumulated during the pandemic, not by income growth. That buffer is now at 2.7 cents per dollar earned. The three-variable stress test ahead - oil above $90 raising gas prices, a potential September rate hike raising variable borrowing costs, and a July jobs report that may show further cooling - lands on a household sector with substantially less cushion than it had at the start of the year.
  • The 13.1% severe credit card delinquency rate - 90-plus-day delinquency, the measure of genuine financial distress rather than short-term cash flow timing - is the household data point most at odds with the "resilient consumer" aggregate narrative. That rate prevailed at the depth of the 2010-2011 post-financial-crisis recovery. It means roughly one in eight credit card dollars is severely past due. The households contributing to that number are not captured in the median PCE spending data; they have already exited the credit market's performing segment and entered its distress segment. A September rate hike - if it materializes - raises the cost of the credit card balances that are not already delinquent and increases the flow of balances into delinquency. The 13.1% rate is not a ceiling.

Bottom Line
  • The KOSPI's 17.91% single-day recovery needs to be read against its 22% monthly decline. The net result is a month that still ended approximately -6 to -7% below where it started - even after today's historic session. The chip sector's July experience is the most concentrated illustration of how forward fear can detach from backward fundamentals in a high-uncertainty environment. Samsung reported record quarterly profits during the July selloff. SK Hynix's memory business is structurally sound. Neither company's fundamental position deteriorated during the three-week chip rout. What deteriorated was the market's confidence in the demand sustainability of the AI buildout - and that confidence has been partially restored by two data points: Microsoft Azure at $100 billion run rate and 43% growth, and Amazon AWS at $42.2 billion and 37% growth. Those two numbers together constitute a demand signal that the selloff was priced for not materializing.
  • Japan's 4.03% session gain is the quieter but perhaps more durable signal from Friday's Asian trading. The Nikkei's recovery reflects a broader point: the AI hardware buildout - wherever the chips are manufactured - generates demand for the Japanese semiconductor-equipment supply chain. ASML, Tokyo Electron, and Advantest are as exposed to AI-demand signals as SK Hynix, just one step removed in the supply chain. Today's Nikkei move confirms that the recovery from this week's fear-driven selloff has a geographic spread beyond Seoul. The question for August is whether the macro headwinds - US rate hike probability, Iran energy risk, China semiconductor decoupling - reassert after the earnings catalyst fades.
