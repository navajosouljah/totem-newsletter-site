Thursday Briefing • Thursday, August 13, 2026

July CPI Matched Consensus at 3.4% - September Hike Odds Fall to 38%. S&P 500 Recovered to 7,748 as Nasdaq Led Relief Rally. Oil Retreats to WTI $82.11 on Hormuz Deal Signals. USDA Corn Yield 180.7 BPA Misses Estimates. Bitcoin $63,402. PPI + Jobless Claims at 8:30 AM ET Today. Retail Sales Tomorrow.

Wednesday delivered what markets needed most: July CPI at 3.4% headline and 0.2% monthly core matched consensus exactly, easing September hike odds from 50% to approximately 38% and lifting equities out of a two-day decline. Oil retreated from near-$90 Brent as President Trump claimed "total control" over the Strait of Hormuz. The USDA set corn yield at 180.7 bushels per acre - below the 182.4 analyst consensus - sending corn and soybean futures sharply higher. Thursday's calendar: PPI and jobless claims at 8:30 AM ET, Freddie Mac PMMS at 12 PM ET.

The Quick Read

The CPI read was clean in both directions. July headline at +3.4% YoY and core at +0.2% MoM matched consensus estimates exactly, with no upside or downside surprise. The 10-year Treasury yield fell approximately 4 basis points to 4.656% after the release - a modest bond-market relief move that confirms the print neither confirmed nor denied the September hike thesis. September hike odds retreated from approximately 50% pre-CPI to approximately 38% after - consistent with a market that sees the in-line read as moderately rate-friendly but not definitively so. Bloomberg captured the state accurately: bond traders are "keeping wagers on a September hike" - the CPI reduced urgency but did not eliminate the possibility.

The equity market's response was calibrated. S&P 500 +0.26% to 7,748.50, recovering approximately two-thirds of the two-day 29-point decline from the August 8 record. Nasdaq led at +0.54% to 26,588.49, consistent with the rate-sensitive sector's outsize response to reduced hike odds. The Dow was essentially flat at 53,770.27 (-0.04%), where energy-sector softness from the oil retreat partially offset the rate relief. Thursday premarket is muted: S&P 500 futures +0.08%, Nasdaq 100 +0.13%, as the market consolidates the CPI relief ahead of secondary data.

Oil's retreat on Wednesday was the second major story. WTI fell from Wednesday morning's $84.17 to approximately $82.11 Thursday morning - a $2/barrel decline of approximately 1.4%. Brent fell from near $90 to $87.92. The proximate cause was a combination of CPI relief (lower September hike odds reduce demand-destruction concern at the margin) and Hormuz deal signals. President Trump stated the US has "total control" over the Strait of Hormuz - a comment oil traders interpreted as both military dominance and negotiating leverage, opening space for a deal framework. The IEA's monthly report simultaneously flagged a 1.8 million barrel per day global oil shortfall this quarter, a number that underscores how much supply has been disrupted even as diplomatic signals circulate. Negotiations formally remain deadlocked; the oil retreat is a signal move, not a supply-resolution move.

The USDA's first official corn yield estimate landed at 180.7 bushels per acre - below both the analyst consensus of 182.4 bpa and USDA's own July estimate of 183 bpa. The number sits within the 179-183 "moderate" range that Wednesday's analysis characterized as "weather premium maintained but not extended." But the 2.3 bpa miss from USDA's own prior estimate was sufficient to send corn and soybean futures sharply higher post-report. Total corn production came in at 16.013 billion bushels, above prior projections due to a 1.4-million-acre increase in harvested area - a partially offsetting factor that prevented the extreme price response that a below-179 yield would have triggered. The food cost channel of August and September CPI is now confirmed to be adding, not subtracting, to inflationary pressure.

A cold PPI (at or below 0.1%) would add a second consecutive inflation-relief data point and push September hike odds toward 30-35%. Jobless claims continuing below 200K (the third-consecutive-week threshold) would signal labor market resilience; a break above 210K would introduce new labor concerns.
