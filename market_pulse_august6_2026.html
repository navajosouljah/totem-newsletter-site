Thursday Briefing • Thursday, August 6, 2026

Dow Hits Third Consecutive All-Time Record at 54,349 While ADP July Payrolls Miss at 44,000 - Worst in Six Months - and September Hike Odds Fall Sharply. Oil Drops 7% to $75 on Hormuz Deal Progress as Houthis Attack Saudi Tanker. AppLovin Crashes 28.7%. ISM Services Employment Contracts to 47.4%. KOSPI -4.58% as Samsung Falls 6% and SK Hynix Loses 10%. Initial Jobless Claims at 8:30 AM ET. BLS July Jobs Report Tomorrow.

Wednesday delivered two data surprises pulling in opposite directions: ADP private payrolls came in at 44,000 for July - the worst reading in six months and below every major forecast - while Treasury Secretary Bessent told CNBC a Strait of Hormuz deal could arrive "today or tomorrow," sending WTI crude from $81 to approximately $75 in a single session. The Dow absorbed both developments with a 263-point gain to a third consecutive record at 54,349.06, driven by the same cyclical and value names that benefit most from lower oil prices. The Nasdaq could not hold: AppLovin's 28.7% earnings crash pulled the index down 0.83%, while the Russell 2000 gained 1.85% in one of the clearest single-day rotations of the summer. ISM Services PMI came in at 54.1% - both halves of the economy expanding simultaneously - but the services employment index fell back into contraction at 47.4%, adding another weak labor signal ahead of tomorrow morning's BLS July jobs report. Initial jobless claims release at 8:30 AM ET today. The jobs report follows at 8:30 AM ET Friday.

The Quick Read

What Wednesday changed: The Dow Jones Industrial Average gained 263.18 points to close at 54,349.06 - a third consecutive all-time record. The S&P 500 slipped 0.17% to 7,723.55 after setting its own record on Tuesday at 7,736.52. The Nasdaq fell 0.83% to 26,363.44 as AppLovin's 28.7% single-session crash - following a revenue report that missed forecasts by 0.8%, to $1.924 billion - became the week's sharpest counterpoint to Palantir's 29% gain on Tuesday. The Russell 2000 gained 1.85%, its strongest day in two weeks. The divergence is the story: investors rotated from large-cap technology into small-caps and cyclicals as oil prices fell and Hormuz deal optimism elevated the growth case for rate-sensitive domestic businesses.

The ADP July private payrolls report landed at 44,000 - the weakest reading in six months, well below the approximately 70,000 consensus forecast, and below every individual estimate on the Street. Services added 47,000 jobs while the goods-producing sector shed 3,000. An hour later, ISM Services PMI confirmed both the expansion and the labor concern: the headline index rose to 54.1% from June's 54.0%, with business activity surging to 59.1% and new orders rising to 57.2% - but the employment component fell from 51.2% to 47.4%, dropping back into contraction territory for the first time since June. Two separate labor market surveys released within hours of each other both signaled the same thing: the US services economy is busy, but it is not adding workers at the pace required to sustain the September rate hike case.

The Strait of Hormuz situation shifted from cautious optimism to imminent-deal framing on Wednesday. Treasury Secretary Bessent told CNBC a Hormuz reopening deal could arrive "today or tomorrow" - language more definitive than anything previously on record from a senior US official. Iran-Oman negotiations had, by Wednesday morning, produced proposed shipping lane coordinates. WTI crude fell from approximately $81 to approximately $75 - a 7% single-session move. The complication arrived within hours: Houthi forces claimed an attack on a Saudi oil tanker on Wednesday and threatened the Bab el-Mandeb strait as a secondary chokepoint. The tanker attack is the market's reminder that Bessent's "today or tomorrow" has been approximately true before in this conflict, and the distance between a proposed framework and an operational reopening involves multiple actors, not all of whom are in the Oman talks. WTI settled near $75; the direction of travel on crude is lower if the deal materializes, but the Houthi complication creates a scenario where oil could snap back if the tanker attack triggers a US response.

Across the Pacific, the Korean semiconductor sector experienced its worst single day of the summer. Samsung Electronics fell 6.3%. SK Hynix lost more than 10%. The KOSPI index dropped 4.58% - erasing a meaningful portion of the 18% historic single-day rebound it posted on July 31. The semiconductor sell-off is not primarily a valuation or earnings story; it reflects concern that the AI hardware buildout driving demand for memory and logic chips may be encountering demand-rate concerns at current inventory levels. This is a fundamentally different signal from AppLovin's single-day miss. AppLovin is a software advertising platform whose revenue missed by 0.8%. Korea's chip sector decline reflects the supply-chain reality check that sits beneath the AI infrastructure spending cycle: the companies that build the hardware enabling AI are subject to inventory cycles, not just to demand expansion.

Gold closed at approximately $4,278 per ounce - up 0.72% on the day, up 4-5% on the week, and above $4,000 for the fifth consecutive session. The coexistence of a Dow record at 54,349 and gold at $4,278 is the market's honest summary of where risk sits: the Dow is pricing Hormuz deal optimism and oil-driven disinflation; gold is pricing the possibility that the deal does not arrive, the Houthi attack escalates, and the ADP print foreshadows a Friday jobs report that reopens the question of whether the US economy is cooling faster than the Fed's current framework assumes. Both readings are internally consistent. They are measuring different scenarios. The market is keeping both open simultaneously.

Thursday Morning

  • ADP July private payrolls: 44,000 - worst reading in six months. The consensus forecast was approximately 70,000. Services sector added 47,000 jobs while goods-producing shed 3,000. June's ADP was a downwardly revised 95,000. The trajectory - 95,000 revised to 44,000 in a single month - is more significant than either number in isolation. The weakest sectors were leisure and hospitality and professional services. ADP's July read is now the single most important forward signal for tomorrow's BLS jobs report: in months when ADP and BLS agree directionally, the BLS number comes in within approximately 30,000 of the ADP. If that relationship holds on Friday, the BLS number is somewhere in the 20,000-70,000 range - well below the 75,000-90,000 consensus.
  • ISM Services PMI July: 54.1% - expansion for the 25th consecutive month, but employment back in contraction. The headline index rose to 54.1% from June's 54.0%, missing the 54.5% forecast by a small margin. Business activity surged 3.7 points to 59.1%. New orders rose 2.1 points to 57.2%. The number that matters most going into Friday: the employment index fell from 51.2% to 47.4% - a 3.8-point drop that pushed it back into contraction territory. Combined with ADP, two independent service-sector surveys on the same day are telling the same story about labor: services activity is high, but hiring is not keeping pace with that activity. The ISM Services employment index has historically led BLS payroll data by two to four weeks.
  • Hormuz deal progress: Bessent says reopening "today or tomorrow" - WTI drops 7% to approximately $75. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told CNBC on Wednesday that a deal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz could arrive "today or tomorrow." Iran-Oman negotiations have produced agreed proposed shipping lane coordinates. The market's response was immediate: WTI fell from approximately $81 to $75 in the session. If realized, a Hormuz reopening would be the most consequential single economic event of the second half of 2026 - removing a risk premium that has added approximately $8-10 per barrel to crude since the crisis began and potentially accelerating the decline in pump prices that households have been waiting for since May.
  • Houthi attack on Saudi tanker complicates the deal picture. On Wednesday, Houthi forces claimed responsibility for attacking a Saudi oil tanker and threatened the Bab el-Mandeb strait as a secondary chokepoint. The attack is the clearest operational signal that regional actors outside the Iran-Oman framework retain the ability to disrupt energy flows even if the Hormuz negotiations succeed. WTI held near $75 through the day, suggesting the market has not yet assigned a high probability to Houthi escalation overriding the Hormuz deal. But the threat exists: if the US responds to the tanker attack with strikes, the deal framework may collapse.
  • AppLovin -28.7% - the mirror image of Palantir's Tuesday triumph. AppLovin reported Q2 revenue of $1.924 billion - 0.8% below the $1.940 billion consensus. The stock lost 28.7% in a single session. Palantir on Tuesday reported $1.935 billion in revenue, beating consensus by approximately $123 million, or 7%, and gained 29%. The arithmetic of this week's two biggest single-stock moves is clear: in a market priced for AI-driven growth, the spread between beating by 7% and missing by 0.8% is approximately 58 percentage points of stock price performance in two days. AppLovin was also the primary driver of the Nasdaq's 0.83% decline on Wednesday; without AppLovin, the Nasdaq would likely have been flat to modestly positive.
  • Dow record at 54,349 and Russell 2000 +1.85% signal a rotation, not a crash. The Dow's third consecutive record close is driven by the same dynamics: Caterpillar, industrials, financials, and energy-adjacent names that benefit from lower crude and a stable-to-improving growth picture. The Russell 2000's 1.85% gain is more instructive for the macro story: small-cap US companies benefit disproportionately from lower oil prices and lower interest rate expectations. When the Russell outperforms the Nasdaq by more than 2.5 percentage points in a single day, the market is making a directional bet about the rate path - and Wednesday's rotation says the ADP print has moved the September hike from likely to uncertain.
  • KOSPI -4.58%, Samsung -6.3%, SK Hynix -10%+: Korean chip stocks enter their worst week of the summer. The semiconductor decline follows the Korean market's 18% historic single-day rebound on July 31. The reversal is sharp: Samsung's loss on Wednesday alone erased more than a week of gains. SK Hynix's 10%+ drop is the more instructive data point - the memory chip market is more sensitive to inventory cycles than logic chips, and a 10% single-day move in the world's second-largest memory producer signals that institutional investors have revised their demand-rate assumptions for AI hardware, not just reacted to a single earnings release.

Bottom Line
  • The Dow at a third consecutive record while the Nasdaq loses 0.83% is not a contradiction - it is a description of a market repricing two things simultaneously. Lower crude prices benefit the Dow's industrial and energy-adjacent composition. Weak labor data reduces the probability of a September rate hike, which benefits rate-sensitive small-cap and cyclical names. The Nasdaq's large-cap technology composition is less directly helped by either dynamic: AI spending is a long-cycle story, and a single weak ADP print does not change the earnings trajectory of the companies most exposed to it. What it changes is valuation. When rate expectations fall, the discount rate falls, and high-multiple growth stocks should benefit - but AppLovin's 28.7% crash on a 0.8% revenue miss is a reminder that the market's tolerance for any miss, at any multiple, is essentially zero in this environment.
  • The Russell 2000's 1.85% gain is the number to watch heading into Friday's jobs report. Small-cap domestic US companies - which are more rate-sensitive, more oil-dependent, and more exposed to the domestic consumer than the mega-cap Nasdaq names - are telling you that the market has begun pricing a softer Fed path. If Friday's BLS number comes in below 60,000, the Russell likely extends gains while the Nasdaq digests the growth concern embedded in the weak payroll number. If Friday comes in above 120,000, the rotation reverses: the rate-hike probability recovers, the Dow gives back some of its three-day run, and the Nasdaq rebounds as rate-driven multiple pressure eases.

ADP +44,000 and ISM Services employment at 47.4% have done what JOLTS alone could not: they have put the September rate hike case on the defensive. The 10-year Treasury and the futures market are both recalibrating toward a hold, with some participants beginning to price the possibility that the next Fed move is a cut rather than a hike.

Fed policy rate: 3.50-3.75% held July 29; 9-3 vote; three dissents in favor of a +25bp hike; next meeting September 15-16
September hike probability (futures market): fell sharply on Wednesday after ADP +44,000 (vs ~70,000 forecast) and ISM Services employment falling to 47.4% (contraction); the hike case was at approximately 55-56% Tuesday morning post-JOLTS; both Wednesday releases move the probability materially lower; initial jobless claims this morning and Friday's BLS number are the remaining inputs before September 15-16
2-year Treasury: ~4.21% the front end is the most direct rate-path gauge; 4.21% reflects a market that has absorbed the JOLTS softness and the ADP miss and is no longer pricing a September hike as the base case; a BLS number above 120,000 tomorrow reverses some of this move; a BLS number below 60,000 takes the 2-year toward 4.00%
10-year Treasury: ~4.63% holding at the lower end of its recent 4.63-4.75% range; oil falling from $81 to $75 eases near-term inflation expectations, which provides some relief to the long end; the 10-year's direction from here depends more on Friday's jobs number than on any other single input this week
Yield curve (10s-2s spread): +42 basis points positively sloped - a meaningful reversal from the inversion that characterized most of 2024 and early 2025; a positive curve implies the bond market sees growth continuing, not an imminent recession; the spread will narrow if Friday's jobs number is very weak (2-year falls faster than the 10-year)

Bottom Line
  • The September rate decision is now genuinely two-sided. The three FOMC dissenters who voted to hike on July 29 built their case on: ISM Manufacturing at 55.6 (4-year high), oil near $90 in mid-July, and a labor market they characterized as "tight enough to sustain price pressure." Two of those three pillars have shifted materially in a week. Oil is now at $75, down approximately $15 from its peak. The labor market has produced JOLTS slightly below consensus, ADP at 44,000 - worst in six months - and ISM Services employment back in contraction. The ISM Manufacturing reading at 55.6 remains the dissenters' strongest remaining argument. If Friday's BLS number is close to the ADP signal (20,000-70,000), the three dissenters' case weakens significantly between now and September 15.
  • Initial jobless claims this morning at 8:30 AM ET are the last weekly labor data point before Friday's BLS release. In a normal week, claims running at 220,000-230,000 are considered consistent with a healthy labor market. In this week's context, a claims reading above 240,000 - reinforcing the ADP and ISM Services employment signals - would move the September hike probability to or below 35%. A reading below 210,000 - the kind that signals labor market strength - would partially restore the hike case and likely reverse some of Wednesday's bond market move. Claims data is more volatile week-to-week than the monthly payroll numbers, but in the current environment every piece of labor data is being interpreted as a direct input to the September decision.

Bottom Line
  • The Hormuz deal timeline matters for energy prices more than any other single variable over the next 30 days. WTI at $75 is already pricing a high probability of a deal. If the deal is announced today or tomorrow as Bessent indicated, the immediate market reaction is likely another $3-5 per barrel decline in crude - taking WTI toward $70-72. At $70 WTI, EIA's models project a national average gas price of approximately $3.50-3.60 by late September. For a household filling a 15-gallon tank weekly, the difference between $4.05 and $3.55 at the pump is approximately $7.50 per fill-up, or roughly $390 per year. That is the household economic value of a Hormuz deal materializing in the next 48 hours.
  • The Houthi tanker attack is the complication the oil market could not have predicted Wednesday morning. The Bab el-Mandeb strait - the southern Red Sea chokepoint connecting the Gulf of Aden to the Red Sea - handles approximately 6.2 million barrels per day of oil traffic. A sustained Houthi campaign against tankers in Bab el-Mandeb, concurrent with a Hormuz reopening, would create a partial offset to the Hormuz-reopening price relief. The market held WTI near $75 through Wednesday's session despite the tanker attack, suggesting it views Bab el-Mandeb as a secondary risk rather than a primary one. But this calculus could shift quickly if the US responds to the tanker attack with strikes that widen the regional conflict.

Gold buyers are central banks executing quarterly allocation mandates, sovereign wealth funds hedging geopolitical scenarios that a Hormuz deal does not fully resolve, and institutional real-rate allocators whose thesis is that the 10-year at 4.63% minus inflation running at 3.5% leaves real rates uncomfortably low even after oil-driven disinflation.   • Silver's 4.61% single-day gain is worth noting separately from gold. Silver moved more than gold on Wednesday because it is pricing two things at once: the geopolitical hedge (safe-haven component) and the Hormuz deal (industrial component, since silver's largest end-uses - solar panels, electronics, semiconductors - benefit from growth optionality). When both components are constructive simultaneously, silver can outperform gold on individual sessions by a wide margin. The question is which component dominates over the next 30 days. If the Hormuz deal materializes and the labor market stabilizes, silver's industrial bid likely sustains. If the deal fails and the jobs report is very soft, the safe-haven bid becomes the sole driver.

Core PCE (ex-food & energy, June): +0.1% MoM +3.3% YoY; the Fed's preferred measure; 1.3 percentage points above target; three consecutive months at or above 3.3%
U of Michigan Sentiment (July final): 55.2 near-12% jump from June; driven by lower gas prices and stock market record closes; at 55.2, still approximately 32 points below the long-run average of 88 - the gap between what financial assets indicate and what the median household reports remains historically wide
Personal savings rate (June): 2.7% spending continues to outrun income at the national level; at ADP's pace of 44,000 new jobs per month, the income growth rate slows, which eventually forces a spending deceleration unless households draw further on savings or credit
Credit card APR (Fed G.19, May): 21.15% the cost structure facing households funding daily expenses on revolving credit; if the September hike probability has fallen as much as Wednesday's data suggests, this number does not rise further in the near term - but it also does not fall until the Fed begins cutting
Bottom Line
  • The ADP print at 44,000 is the most direct household finance data point of the week because it is the leading indicator for income growth. In an economy where household spending has been outrunning income growth (savings rate 2.7%), the source of the gap is either accumulated excess savings being drawn down or credit (APR 21.15%) being used to bridge the shortfall. At 44,000 new jobs per month, neither of those bridges gets rebuilt - savings continue to decline and credit balances continue to grow. If Friday's BLS print confirms the ADP signal, the household finance stress that has been building since late 2025 has a cleaner trend line: fewer jobs being added, savings continuing to draw down, and credit costs that will not fall until the Fed pivots.
  • The oil price drop is the one immediate positive for household budgets in this week's data. WTI at $75 versus $93 in mid-July represents a $18 per barrel decline. With the 2-to-4 week pump lag, households in the Gulf Coast region may see gas approach $3.40-3.50 by late August - down from approximately $3.70 now. At a national level, a sustained $75 WTI would take pump prices toward $3.75-3.85. For the median household spending approximately $130 per month on gas, a move from $4.05 to $3.80 saves roughly $60 per month. That is real purchasing power relief - but it is not a substitute for income growth, and it does not reduce credit card balances or slow foreclosure starts.

The rate path is the primary macro variable for crypto's next directional move: if Friday's jobs report pushes the 10-year toward 4.40-4.50%, the improved risk environment and reduced rate headwinds are a genuine positive for crypto flows. Around the World

The Korean semiconductor sell-off is the international story that connects most directly to the AI investment thesis. KOSPI's 4.58% decline on Samsung -6.3% and SK Hynix -10%+ is not a Korea-specific story - it is a global AI hardware supply chain recalibration.

Nikkei 225 (Wednesday Aug 5): 65,683.26 -0.93%; reversed some of Tuesday's 3.66% gain; semiconductor weakness (Tokyo Electron -5.5%, Advantest -2.34%) weighed; Japan continues to trade with elevated volatility tied to its semiconductor export and AI hardware exposure
KOSPI (Wednesday Aug 5): 6,296.38 -4.58%; Samsung Electronics -6.3%; SK Hynix -10%+; worst single session since August 1; the KOSPI had recovered 18% in a historic single-day rebound on July 31 - Wednesday's loss erases a meaningful portion of that recovery in a single session
Samsung Electronics (Samsung): -6.3% on August 5; Samsung is the world's largest memory chip producer; its single-day loss reflects institutional assessment that memory chip demand rates are encountering inventory concerns, not just near-term earnings variability
SK Hynix (HBM leader): -10%+; SK Hynix produces the high-bandwidth memory (HBM) chips that power AI training hardware; a 10%+ decline in the world's leading HBM producer is the sharpest single data point this week about whether AI hardware demand is sustaining at the pace that drove Q2 earnings
European markets (Wednesday): DAX, FTSE, CAC 40 all modestly higher, approximately +0.2-0.3%; European markets absorbed the Hormuz deal optimism more cleanly than Asia, partly because Europe's manufacturing sector benefits directly from lower energy prices
Brazil: Copom (central bank) delivered its fourth consecutive rate cut, lowering the Selic rate to 14.00%; Brazil's rate cycle is ahead of the US by approximately 12-18 months - a potential preview of where developed market rate paths could go if labor data continues to soften
China Manufacturing PMI (July official): 49.2 first contraction in five months; weak domestic demand; as US ISM Manufacturing sits at 55.6 and China contracts at 49.2, the manufacturing divergence between the two largest economies is widening
Bottom Line
  • The SK Hynix 10%+ decline is the single most consequential international data point of the week for the AI investment thesis. SK Hynix is not a peripheral AI hardware company - it is the primary supplier of the high-bandwidth memory that makes AI training hardware function at the speeds required for large language model development. When Nvidia, AMD, or any other AI chip company builds a processor for AI workloads, the HBM stacked on that processor comes primarily from SK Hynix. A 10% single-session decline in SK Hynix is institutional money reassessing the rate of demand growth for AI hardware at the component level. It does not mean AI spending is ending. It means the inventory build-up cycle that accompanies rapid demand growth is producing the same kind of correction that semiconductor cycles always produce: supply catches up, inventory rises, prices and margins compress, stocks correct. That correction is now underway in the memory segment.
  • The China-US manufacturing divergence continued to widen on Wednesday. US ISM Manufacturing at 55.6 (four-year high, published August 3) versus China official PMI at 49.2 (contraction). The two readings describe the same global manufacturing cycle from opposite sides: AI infrastructure spending, semiconductor supply chains, and power infrastructure investment are concentrated in the US, Taiwan, and South Korea. Chinese manufacturers face a domestic demand shortfall and trade headwinds simultaneously. Caterpillar's earnings raise on Tuesday - citing AI data center construction as a demand driver - is the direct equipment link between US factory expansion and where global infrastructure dollars are landing. China is not a beneficiary of this cycle in the same way the US manufacturing sector is.
