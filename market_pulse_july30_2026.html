Weekly Briefing • Thursday, July 30, 2026

GDP Grows 1.5% in Q2 as Headline PCE Posts First Monthly Price Drop in Six Years. Markets Recover - S&P +1.1%, Nasdaq +2.6% Midday. Microsoft +15% on Azure's $100B Run Rate. Freddie Mac 30-Year Rises to 6.66%. Apple and Amazon After the Close Tonight.

The Quick Read

Three votes for a hike is not a close call - it is a policy signal. The framing of Wednesday's FOMC as a "hold" is technically accurate and substantively misleading. When three of twelve voting members advocate raising rates at a meeting where the other nine vote to hold, the committee is not in neutral. It is in a configuration where the hawkish minority is organized, named, and on the record. Hammack, Kashkari, and Logan did not dissent quietly; they dissented with a specific prescription: rates need to go higher. The absence of a cut dissent - zero members advocated for easing - tells you the distribution of opinion within the committee has shifted entirely to the right of the rate dial. September is not a cut meeting. The question is whether it is a hold meeting or a hike meeting.

The 80% September cut probability that markets had priced heading into Wednesday did not reflect a considered read of the committee's internal distribution. It reflected a consensus that had built over weeks on the logic of oil prices declining, inflation trending down, and a historically cautious chair. All three of those inputs remain partially valid - oil had been declining, inflation had been trending lower, and Warsh is temperamentally deliberate. But the hike votes reframe each one. If three members of the FOMC looked at the same oil decline, the same inflation trend, and the same chair and concluded rates need to go up, then either they have information markets don't, or they weigh the available data very differently. Wednesday's press conference suggests the latter: the dissenters appear to be reacting to the persistence of core services inflation and the labor market's refusal to soften enough to justify further patience.

The Microsoft-Meta split is the AI-spending verdict that the past two weeks of chip-sector turbulence was waiting for. Microsoft demonstrated that AI capital expenditure can produce near-term revenue acceleration - Azure's $100 billion run rate and 43% growth rate are the evidence. Meta demonstrated the opposite: $31 billion in a single quarter of AI CapEx produced a margin collapse, not a revenue surge proportional to the spend. The distinction matters for how the market prices the entire AI-infrastructure trade going forward. If Microsoft's result is the template - spend heavily, see accelerating cloud revenue, CapEx below forecast - the AI thesis survives the chip-rout scrutiny. If Meta's result - spend more than expected, miss earnings, compress margins - is the template, the thesis requires a longer time horizon than equity markets can comfortably price. Microsoft's final Thursday gain of +15% - well above the +8% premarket reading - signals that markets are treating Azure's $100 billion run rate as the more important number and sidelining Meta's margin story for now.

The 8:30 AM ET data releases landed on the dovish side of the range the market had feared. Q2 GDP came in at +1.5% annualized - slightly below the +1.8% consensus but matching the Atlanta Fed's GDPNow forecast - confirming that growth is slowing without collapsing. More importantly, June headline PCE fell -0.1% month-over-month, the first monthly price decline in six years, driven by lower energy prices. Core PCE came in at +0.1% MoM and +3.3% YoY - in line with estimates and down from May's +3.4%. Personal income rose +0.2% MoM ($54.9 billion) while personal spending increased +0.3% MoM ($65.2 billion), leaving the personal savings rate at 2.7%. Markets read the combined GDP-plus-PCE print as incrementally dovish: a slowing economy with decelerating inflation gives the Fed less justification for the September hike that three committee members already voted for. By midday, the S&P 500 had recovered +1.1% to approximately 7,422, the Nasdaq +2.6% to approximately 25,081, and the Dow +0.8% to approximately 52,010. The Russell 2000 declined -1.6%, reflecting small-cap sensitivity to the rate-shock from Wednesday's FOMC even as large-cap tech rallied on Microsoft's results.

  • Credit card delinquency at 13.1% - 90-plus-day delinquency, the measure of genuine financial strain rather than short-term cash flow management - is the household finance number that most directly contradicts the "resilient consumer" narrative. At 13.1%, roughly one in eight credit card dollars is severely delinquent. That rate prevailed at the depth of the 2010-2011 recovery from the financial crisis. The households contributing to that number are not the median consumer that PCE or savings rate data describes; they are the households who absorbed $4-plus gas, 23.8% credit card APRs, $777 monthly car payments, and now face a September hike rather than a September cut. The institutional investor who studies the aggregate PCE print and concludes the consumer is fine is looking at the average. The 13.1% delinquency rate is looking at the tail - and the tail is what creates systemic risk.

If Microsoft's result confirms that AI capital expenditure generates measurable near-term returns, the broader technology risk-premium compresses - and crypto trades as a technology-adjacent risk asset when the macro environment allows. If Meta's margin collapse story dominates investor thinking over the next 48 hours, the AI-skepticism narrative that drove the Korean chip selloff this week re-emerges, and crypto faces a risk-off impulse from both the rate shock and the earnings narrative. Tonight's Apple and Amazon results will clarify which story the market treats as the template heading into August.

Bottom Line
  • The Kospi's third consecutive session of losses - even as Samsung reports the largest quarterly operating profit in the company's history - is the clearest signal yet that the AI-demand narrative is being weighed against the supply-chain concentration risk narrative in real time. Samsung's 1,813% year-over-year profit increase reflects the AI hardware buildout at its most direct. The Kospi's decline reflects the market's response to two weeks of evidence that the AI buildout's sustainability is uncertain, that China's CXMT is building domestic alternatives, and that the largest AI spender (Meta) just posted a margin collapse. A company can report record profits and a declining stock simultaneously only when investors have decided the forward view has changed more than the backward earnings indicate.
  • The DXY moving from ~101.5 to ~102-103 in a single FOMC session is a global macro event, not just a US rate story. A stronger dollar at this pace affects every dollar-denominated commodity price, every EM debt service schedule, and every multinational earnings translation in the next quarter. Oil above $90 plus a stronger dollar is a particularly acute combination for oil-importing EMs - the bill rises in both raw price and currency terms simultaneously. The four-day pause in Iran strikes had briefly created a window where EMs could anticipate relief on both dimensions. That window closed overnight. The EM stress that had been building since July 21 is now back on an accelerating trajectory with fewer near-term relief catalysts than there were 24 hours ago.
