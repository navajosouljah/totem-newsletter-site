The Demand Illusion: AI Chips' 80% Rally and OpenAI's $20 Billion Gap

Chipmakers are up more than 80% this year on a forecast of nearly bottomless AI demand. On Thursday, OpenAI's revenue turned out to be about $20 billion below what had been signaled, and the whole trade flinched. The growth is real and the companies are extraordinary. We challenge the one word doing all the work in the pitch: demand.

10–15 minute read Share

Every issue, we analyze the published strategies of prominent voices in the wealth-building space. Where we agree, we say so. Where we differ, we run both strategies through our six-question Totem Scorecard and publish the comparison. This week we take on the loudest growth trade on the feed: AI stocks, the chipmakers, cloud builders, and data center names priced on a forecast of artificial intelligence demand that only ever seems to get bigger.

This Week's Target

For the fourth issue running, the target is not a single commentator. It is a consensus, and right now it is the most crowded trade in the market. Chipmakers have soared more than 80% so far this year. In Bank of America's September survey of fund managers, "long global semiconductors" was named the most crowded trade for a third straight month, after a record 82% of managers picked it in July.

This week's news is a single number. On Thursday the Financial Times reported, and CNBC confirmed, that OpenAI told investors its annualized revenue was roughly $50 billion at the end of September, against the $68 billion figure widely reported late last month. Nvidia fell 3%, Oracle nearly 6%, and CoreWeave nearly 8%, and the Nasdaq posted its biggest one-day loss since mid-August.

The challenged piece: the AI demand pitch of 2026, distilled in Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang's March forecast of $1 trillion in demand for its Blackwell and Vera Rubin chips through 2027 - "double from just a few months ago," as Jim Cramer put it - and restated this week in OpenAI's investor presentation touting 77% run-rate growth in its third quarter.

The AI Trade Playbook

Strip away the tickers and every version of the argument runs the same five-step machine:

  1. Start with a forecast. A trillion dollars of chip demand, a revenue run rate, a capex budget for next year.
  2. Point to the spenders. Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, and Meta are guiding to roughly $720 billion to $745 billion of capital spending in 2026.
  3. Price the future today. The share price absorbs years of expected growth before it is earned.
  4. Finance the gap. When the spending outruns the cash, bonds and loans fill the difference.
  5. Call it demand. The forecast, the budget, and the order book all get described with the same word, as if they were the same thing.

Inside the Playbook

The rally is real. Chipmakers are up more than 80% in 2026, and the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index is on track for its best year since 1999. The spending behind it is real too. The four largest hyperscalers spent a combined $165.1 billion on property and equipment in the second quarter alone, up 87% from a year earlier, and their July guidance implies roughly $720 billion to $745 billion for 2026, against $376 billion of cash capex in 2025. On Thursday, Samsung reported a 783% jump in third-quarter operating profit, and TSMC reported record third-quarter revenue, up 50% from a year earlier.

The forecast moved $20 billion in a day. OpenAI's annualized revenue is "about $20bn less than has been previously signalled," the Financial Times reported, "a massive gap likely to damp optimism about the growth of AI demand." The difference was accounting, not lost sales: the larger figure included gross revenue from OpenAI's partners, built to compare against Anthropic, which counts sales through its cloud partners. That is exactly the point. The demand number the whole trade leans on changed by roughly $20 billion depending on who was counting. AI stocks fell across the board, and in Australia the Nvidia-backed data center operator Firmus withdrew an IPO that had reportedly been set to raise $5 billion, citing market volatility.

The spending is increasingly borrowed. In the second quarter, the four hyperscalers' capex consumed 96% of their combined operating cash flow, and Alphabet and Amazon both spent more than they generated. Alphabet raised $56.2 billion of net debt in the first half of 2026, and Amazon issued $67.0 billion of long-term debt. This week the Wall Street Journal reported that Broadcom was seeking $50 billion in financing and that SpaceX planned $30 billion of investment-grade bonds plus $10 billion in loans, much of it to buy high-end AI chips. Credit default insurance on SpaceX jumped to record highs.

The crowd is the risk. The chip index has already shown what happens when slightly-less-good news meets a crowded trade. It closed July 17 more than 20% below its late-June record, a bear market that took about fifteen trading sessions, while TSMC was reporting a record quarter. Marvell and Arm each lost more than 30% from their highs. In September, fund managers still ranked the semiconductor trade as the most crowded in markets, and 28% named an AI bubble as a top tail risk, second only to a disorderly rise in bond yields.

Price is not payment. A share of a chipmaker is a claim on whatever the market decides that company's future is worth, recalculated every second the exchange is open. The company may have a record order book. The shareholder does not own the order. The shareholder owns the multiple, and the multiple is set by the next forecast.

Where We Second the AI Bulls

There is plenty here we will defend.

The technology is real, and so are the earnings. This is not a story stock with no revenue. Samsung and TSMC just printed record quarters, and analysts expect the S&P 500 to post a 30.6% increase in third-quarter earnings, with tech and energy expected to post the biggest growth, according to LSEG data.

A lot of the demand is contracted. Microsoft reports $678 billion of commercial remaining performance obligation, AWS a $496 billion backlog, and Oracle $664 billion. Google Cloud revenue grew 82% in the second quarter and AWS grew 37%, its fastest pace in 18 quarters. Those backlogs are the closest thing in this trade to a purchase order, and we respect them.

OpenAI is still growing fast. The same presentation that cut the headline number touted 77% run-rate growth in the third quarter and 107% for its enterprise business. The $68 billion figure was a methodology question, not a collapse in sales.

For a patient holder, this has been the year to own it. Someone who bought the chip index in January and sat through July is up more than 80%. Even the broad market has gotten cheaper while rising: Liberty Mutual's Vlad Barbalat notes the S&P 500's forward P/E has fallen from roughly 22x to 19x on strong earnings growth. In a regime where the forecasts keep coming true, the AI trade beats a 5% cycle by a mile, and we will not pretend otherwise.

Where We Challenge: The Demand Illusion

Our singular point of divergence is the word in the forecast.

A forecast is what a company expects to sell. Demand, the kind you can fund, is what a buyer has already ordered. The AI pitch uses one word for both, and the gap between them is the whole story.

The gap has three leaks. The forecast is not an order: a trillion dollars of chip demand through 2027 is a projection, and this week showed that even a revenue run rate can move by $20 billion depending on the method. The buyer is borrowing: when the biggest customers are spending 96% of their operating cash and raising tens of billions in debt to keep buying, the demand depends on credit markets staying open. And the price has already paid for it: a crowded trade does not need bad news to fall, only slightly-less-good news, which is how the chip index lost a fifth of its value in July during a record earnings season.

"A forecast is not a purchase order."

Now run the money. Put $100,000 into the chip index at its late-June record. By July 17, after about fifteen trading sessions, it is worth less than $80,000, and nothing in the companies' results told you it was coming. Put $100,000 into CoreWeave at Wednesday's close and on Thursday it is worth roughly $92,000, on a number about somebody else's revenue. Hold through all of it and you may well end the year far ahead, as January buyers are today. But on any given day, what you have is not a return. It is a price, and the price is a forecast.

Final Thought

The AI trade is the purest example we have covered of a number that sounds impressive until you ask what it is. A trillion dollars of demand is a forecast. A run rate is a method. A share price is today's guess about all of it. None of that makes the companies less remarkable. It just means the word doing the work is a hope with a ticker.
