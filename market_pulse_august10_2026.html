The Quick Read

The week of August 10 has one event today, one event Wednesday, and one event in six weeks - and all three are now connected. Today's USDA Crop Progress at 4:00 PM ET is the fifth consecutive weekly corn rating. A fifth consecutive decline from the 61% good-to-excellent reading of August 2 would validate the weather premium built into corn futures; stability or improvement at 61% would be a mild positive. Wednesday's 8:30 AM ET release brings July CPI and the USDA's first official corn yield estimate simultaneously - these two prints together will either confirm the September rate-hold narrative that has driven the S&P 500 to records or reopen the September hike debate that the Fed's three dissenters have kept alive. And the week of September 15 - which already contained the FOMC meeting - now also contains the Senate floor vote on the CLARITY Act, after Senate Majority Leader Thune filed a cloture motion on Saturday, August 8. Three different market outcomes will converge in a 48-hour window on September 15-16: a Fed rate decision, a Senate vote on the most consequential crypto legislation in US history, and whatever September's rate-path expectations have become after Wednesday's dual print and six weeks of intervening data.

The equity market's setup entering Monday is unusual by historical standards. A record S&P 500 at 7,757.64 is being sustained by a labor market that just posted its first monthly payrolls decline of the year - a -23,000 BLS print that was 106,000 below consensus. The reconciliation is the rate channel: bad economic data is being interpreted as reducing the Fed's justification for the 40% probability September hike, which lifts equity present values. That logic works until the rate-hold thesis is tested by an inflation print. Wednesday is that test. A below-consensus core CPI - the 0.1-0.2% monthly range - extends the rate-hold thesis and likely pushes the S&P above 7,800. An above-consensus core CPI in the 0.4% range restores September hike odds toward 55-60% and tests whether the equity market can hold its record levels when the trade that drove it reverses. There is no data between now and Wednesday morning that materially changes the probability calculation.

The corn crop's condition entering the USDA's first official yield estimate on Wednesday is the less-discussed but equally consequential half of the Wednesday double-header. Four consecutive weeks of declining good-to-excellent ratings - from 67% on July 13 to 61% on August 2 - have built a weather premium into corn futures near $4.39-4.42 per bushel. Today's Crop Progress confirms whether a fifth consecutive decline occurred or whether the crop has stabilized. Wednesday's first official yield estimate - calibrated to a baseline of approximately 181 bushels per acre - is the moment the weather premium either gets validated (yield below 179 bu/acre) or collapses (yield above 183 bu/acre). Because food inflation is one of three arguments the three FOMC dissenters are making for a September hike, the corn yield and the CPI arrive at the same moment on the same morning and will be read together by the same market participants.

Bitcoin's $854 million weekly ETF inflow total - the strongest week since the post-launch period of late 2025 - is the quiet institutional story of a market week dominated by payrolls and Hormuz. BlackRock's IBIT captured $694 million (81% of the total) over five consecutive days that included a semiconductor rout, a payrolls miss, and a 464-point Dow drop. The fact that not one of those days produced a net ETF outflow is the institutional Bitcoin behavior that distinguishes 2026 from every prior crypto cycle: the buyers are not reacting to macro news flow. They are running a capital allocation program. Monday's $65,200 Bitcoin price - up from $64,745 at Friday's close - enters a week where the CLARITY Act's September 15 floor vote has shifted from "theoretically possible" to "scheduled."

Bottom Line
  • For housing, the sequence from here is straightforward but not fast. Wednesday's CPI is step one: a cold print produces a lower 10-year yield that Thursday's Freddie Mac PMMS captures as a 6.60-6.65% reading. That improvement is worth approximately $20-25 per month on a $400,000 mortgage. A subsequent cold inflation reading in September - which would arrive before the FOMC meeting - produces another step down. Three sequential cold inflation readings, from July through September, would be the scenario that takes mortgage rates from 6.69% to 6.30-6.40% by October, which is when traditional fall selling season peaks. That sequence begins Wednesday. Whether it continues depends on each subsequent month's data - there is no short-cut from 6.69% to 6.30% in a single data release.

Household Finance

The household finance picture entering the week combines the S&P 500 at a record (portfolio wealth up for equity holders), gas prices approaching $4.00 nationally (broadly distributed benefit), credit card APRs unchanged at 22.18% (no relief until cuts begin), and July consumer sentiment at its highest since February. The K-shaped divergence between asset-holders and debt-holders remains the defining household dynamic.

University of Michigan Consumer Sentiment (July final, July 31): 55.2 revised up from 54.0 preliminary; up from 49.5 in June; highest since February 2026; improved across all income, age, and education groups; still approximately 11% below year-earlier levels; the improvement alongside a record S&P 500 reflects the asset-holder effect - households watching 401(k) balances reach new highs report improved conditions even as the labor market contracted at the margin in July
June retail sales: $768.6 billion +0.2% month-over-month, +6.7% year-over-year; consumer spending has been resilient through the rate cycle; July retail sales release Thursday August 14 - the next meaningful consumer spending read, capturing household behavior during Hormuz's oil price spike and partial recovery
Gas prices (approximately August 10): approximately $3.99-4.02/gallon approaching the psychologically significant $4.00 threshold; approximately half of US states are now below $4.00/gallon; the one consumer benefit that distributes roughly equally across income groups; Indiana, South Carolina, and Tennessee are among the lowest at approximately $3.49-3.58
Credit card APR (composite avg): approximately 22.18% unchanged; will not decline until the Fed cuts rates; with September at 40-42% hike probability (not cut probability), APR relief for revolving-balance households remains 12-18 months away at the earliest; households carrying balances continue to pay over $220 per year per $1,000 of revolving debt - a structurally elevated cost that gas price relief does not offset for the most indebted households
Personal savings rate (June): 2.7% spending continues to outrun income at the national level; the -23,000 July payrolls print means income growth is decelerating at exactly the moment household debt costs are at cycle highs; a savings rate below 3% provides minimal buffer if the labor market continues to contract into August
Thursday August 14 - July retail sales: the first consumer spending read after Hormuz's oil price spike and the -23,000 payrolls miss; whether consumers were absorbing higher costs during the peak-oil period while maintaining spending volume or cutting back will be visible in Thursday's release; retail sales typically move equity markets 0.3-0.5% on a significant miss or beat
Bottom Line
  • Consumer sentiment at 55.2 - a five-month high - is driven by households watching their 401(k) balances reach records on a week the S&P 500 hit an all-time high. It is not driven by households whose July income statement included a job separation notice. The Michigan survey captures a real improvement for the group experiencing the record equity market; it is absent for the group experiencing the contracting labor market. Both groups are real. Both are large. Gas prices approaching $4.00 nationally - down roughly $0.10 from a week ago and roughly $0.10 from two weeks ago - is the one data point this week that applies to both groups in approximately equal measure. For the 120 million US households with a vehicle, that $0.10-per-gallon improvement is real money: approximately $20-25 monthly for the average-miles-driven household, which compounds across the summer.
