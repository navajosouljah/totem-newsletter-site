The Rally Illusion: Oil Funds Up 111% While the Money Heads for the Exit

Energy commodity ETFs are up 111% this year, and Brent crude traded above $112 a barrel on Thursday morning. Over the last 90 days, about $985 million still walked out of those same funds. The run is real. The headlines are real. We challenge the one word doing all the work in the pitch: rally.

10–15 minute read Share

Every issue, we analyze the published strategies of prominent voices in the wealth-building space. Where we agree, we say so. Where we differ, we run both strategies through our six-question Totem Scorecard and publish the comparison. This week we take on the loudest energy trade on the feed: oil funds, sold as the way to ride a supply shock - even as the government's own forecasters expect the price to fall from here.

This Week's Target

For the sixth issue running, the target is not a single commentator. It is a consensus, and this fall it is the loudest trade on the screen. Oil is spiking. Oil funds are how you ride it. When the strait closes, you buy more.

The numbers are not subtle. ETF Action's weekly commodities report for October 17 put the energy category up 5.92% for the week, 111.15% year to date and 115.34% over one year. The United States Brent Oil Fund (BNO), which holds Brent futures, returned 140.1% over the year to October 21. On Thursday morning, October 22, Brent rose 3.1% to $112.16 a barrel while WTI traded near $99.66.

The challenged piece: the 2026 oil-rally pitch - buy an oil fund because the supply shock has further to run - repeated every time Brent jumps, even as the flows and the official forecasts point the other way.

The Oil Rally Playbook

Strip away the tanker footage and every version of the argument runs the same five-step machine:

  1. Name the shock. A closed strait, a struck pipeline, a tanker under fire.
  2. Point to the chart. Triple-digit Brent and a triple-digit year-to-date return do the selling.
  3. Package the access. A commodity ETF turns a futures contract into a ticker you can buy before lunch.
  4. Extrapolate the move. If it ran 111% this year, the next leg must be close.
  5. Call it a rally. The word implies a direction. It says nothing about the exit.

Inside the Playbook

The run is real. Nobody is faking these numbers. ETF Action's October 17 report ranked energy first among all commodity categories for the week, with oil-linked funds in the top spots: USO up 9.15% and DBO up 7.71%. BNO's 140.1% one-year gain is a real fund result as of October 21, on $846 million in net assets.

The money is leaving anyway. The same ETF Action table shows the energy category with net outflows of $83 million over five days, $384 million over 30 days and $985 million over 90 days, and $263 million out year to date, on $3.97 billion in category assets. The report itself called out the divergence: holders trimmed energy exposure despite the strong rally. The price went up. The money went out.

The forecasters see lower prices. The U.S. Energy Information Administration's October Short-Term Energy Outlook, released October 6, raised its call to $105 a barrel for Brent in the fourth quarter. It also expects Brent to average $84 in 2027 and to fall to $74 by the fourth quarter of 2027 as Middle East flows recover and inventories rebuild. Brent averaged $114 in September. The official base case puts the peak behind us, not ahead.

The exit can come in one day. On April 7, Brent and WTI fell toward $95 a barrel after a ceasefire announcement - their largest single-day decline since 2020. Ten days later, on April 17, Brent settled down 9.07% to $90.38 when Iran said the Strait of Hormuz was open. The rally ended on a headline, not a schedule.

A futures fund is not a barrel. BNO is measured against the near-month ICE Brent futures contract, and it charges 1.15% a year. A holder owns a position that rolls from contract to contract and is priced every trading day. When the headline changes, the position changes with it.

Where We Second the Oil Bulls

There is plenty here we will defend.

The shock is physical. The EIA describes attacks on Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline, constrained export routes and record tanker rates in September. This is not a story invented for a chart.

Inventories are falling. The EIA estimates global oil inventories fell by 1.9 million barrels per day in the third quarter and will fall another 0.7 million barrels per day in the fourth. Tight supply is a real argument.

Access is real. Oil ETFs trade every market day and let a saver take a view on energy without opening a futures account. For a short-term view, that convenience is the point.

If the disruption deepens, oil wins. The EIA raised its fourth-quarter Brent forecast by $14 in a single month. If flows stay shut longer than expected, a fund tied to front-month Brent can keep climbing, and over that stretch it can beat a 5% cycle by a wide margin. An honest scorecard has to leave room for that regime.

Where We Challenge: The Rally Illusion

Our singular point of divergence is the word in the pitch.

A rally describes what already happened. The pitch sells it as what comes next. This fall showed that oil funds can post a triple-digit year while close to $1 billion leaves them over 90 days, and while the agency that tracks the market forecasts lower prices from here.

The gap has three leaks. The return is in the rear-view mirror: 111% year to date is what earlier buyers earned, not what a buyer at $112 is offered. The exit is a headline: April delivered two single-day drops of roughly 9% or more within ten days. And the direction is contested: the EIA's base case has Brent averaging $84 next year, about 25% below Thursday's $112.16.

"A rally is a record of the last move. It is not a schedule for the next one."

Now run the money. Put $100,000 into front-month Brent exposure the day before April 17. A 9.07% one-day decline leaves about $90,930 before fees and tracking differences. Or buy at Thursday's $112.16 and let the spot price drift to the EIA's 2027 average of $84, and the same $100,000 marks near $74,900 - an illustration of the official forecast, not a prediction of ours. None of that makes the supply shock fake. It makes the word rally do more work than the forecast can support.
