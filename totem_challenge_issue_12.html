The Liquidity Illusion: Private Credit's 9% Income and the 5% Gate

Non-traded private credit funds pay around 9% and promise a quarterly way out. This quarter, investors at the biggest funds asked for two to three times what the gates allow. The income is real and the managers are serious. We challenge the one word doing all the work in the pitch: liquidity.

10–15 minute read Share

Every issue, we analyze the published strategies of prominent voices in the wealth-building space. Where we agree, we say so. Where we differ, we run both strategies through our six-question Totem Scorecard and publish the comparison. This week we take on the most crowded income trade in wealth management: non-traded private credit funds, the evergreen business development companies sold to individual investors as steady monthly income with a quarterly exit.

This Week's Target

For the third issue running, the target is not a single commentator. It is a product category, run by the biggest names in finance: Blackstone's $77.2 billion Blackstone Private Credit Fund (BCRED), the roughly $26 billion Apollo Debt Solutions fund, Morgan Stanley's North Haven Private Income Fund, Cliffwater's flagship private credit fund, and Goldman Sachs Private Credit Corp.

This week's news is the third-quarter tender season. Fund after fund reported redemption requests well above the 5% it will buy back. On September 28, SEC staff issued a statement calling private credit the area of private-asset valuation that "requires particular care."

The challenged piece: the evergreen private credit pitch of 2026, a fund "designed to deliver durable income and attractive risk-adjusted returns across market cycles" with a quarterly share repurchase program, as presented in BCRED's September 3 letter to investors and across the category's marketing.

The Private Credit Playbook

Strip away the logos and every fund in the category runs the same five-step machine:

  1. Lend to private companies. Mostly senior secured, floating-rate loans to mid-size and large borrowers that never trade on an exchange.
  2. Pay the income monthly. Roughly 9% annualized at the flagship funds.
  3. Mark the book by model. Loans that do not trade get valued with unobservable inputs. Accountants call these Level 3.
  4. Offer a quarterly window. Repurchases of up to 5% of shares, subject to board approval, filled pro rata when more investors ask.
  5. Call it liquidity. The fund never ends, so the window is the only way out, and the pitch presents the window as the exit.

Inside the Playbook

The income is real. BCRED reports a 9.0% annualized total net return for its Class I shares since its 2021 inception and pays a 9.1% annualized distribution rate. Goldman's private credit fund reports roughly 9.4% total return since inception through August 31. These are serious funds run by serious lenders, and the numbers are disclosed.

The window is not the door. Thirteen non-traded NAV BDCs, about 77% of the market by Robert A. Stanger & Co.'s measure, have reported their third quarter so far. They received $9.6 billion in redemption requests, equal to 10.1% of their net asset value, down from 11.2% in the second quarter. They filled about 47% of those requests and left about $5.1 billion unmet. At the fund level, investors asked to redeem 14.7% of Apollo Debt Solutions against a 5% cap, about 16% of Cliffwater's flagship fund against 5%, and 11.4% of Morgan Stanley's North Haven fund, more than double the 5% it plans to buy back. BCRED took about $4.3 billion of requests, roughly 10% of shares, and will fill 5%.

The queue compounds. In the second quarter, BCRED filled about half of $4.5 billion requested and left a $2.3 billion backlog, "a significant portion of which were resubmitted in Q3." By Blackstone's own estimate, an investor who asked for all of their money in both quarters will have received about 75% of it within roughly 90 days. Stanger's chairman put it plainly: "Redemption demand is easing across most reporting funds, but at several of the largest, requests remain backlogged and persisting." Eight funds had not yet reported. One of them, Blue Owl Technology Income Corp., saw second-quarter requests equal to 38.1% of its NAV.

The mark is a model. SEC staff noted that private credit inside registered funds grew nearly 60%, from $170 billion in December 2020 to $270 billion in December 2025. They described the asset as "typically illiquid, individually negotiated loans that do not trade on established secondary markets and therefore generally lack readily available quoted prices." BCRED discloses its debt book marked at 95.4 on average, with the bottom 5% of its private loans marked at 63.4 and non-accruals at 2.2% of cost. All of that is honest disclosure, and every one of those numbers is an estimate until a borrower pays or a buyer bids.

The payout has fine print. BCRED's own footnote says its distributions are not assured and "have been and may in the future be funded through sources other than net investment income." Back in Issue 02, Jamie Dimon warned that private credit values had not yet caught up with the volatility of early 2026. Three quarters of capped tenders later, the regulator is asking the same question in writing.

Where We Second the Private Credit Crowd

There is plenty here we will defend.

Income is the right obsession. Turning capital into recurring cash flow, instead of waiting on paper gains, is the correct instinct. It is the same instinct Totem is built on.

Lending to real businesses beats betting on sentiment. BCRED spreads its book across more than 640 borrowers, runs 0.8x leverage, and reports interest coverage of 2.3x. This is professional underwriting, not a meme.

The gate is disclosed, and it is doing its job. Nobody hid the 5% cap. Stanger's Kevin Gannon said the tender-offer framework was operating as intended, balancing investors who want out against investors who stay. Goldman's fund shows what a matched investor base looks like: requests of 2% in the third quarter, down from 4.99% in the first.

Lenders hold the pricing power right now. Goldman reports spreads about 25 basis points wider than at year-end 2025, with tighter covenants and stronger lender remedies becoming common. For a patient holder who will not need the money early, this is the regime the product was built for.

Where We Challenge: The Liquidity Illusion

Our singular point of divergence is the word in the pitch deck.

A repurchase program is what a fund offers. Liquidity is what the holder gets on the day they ask. The pitch sells the first as if it were the second, and the gap between them is the whole story.

The gap has three leaks. The exit is shared: when requests run past the cap, every seller gets a pro rata slice and a place in line for next quarter. The price is estimated: the NAV you exit at is the fund's best model of loans that do not trade, which is exactly what the SEC just asked managers to tighten. And the payout is not the profit: a distribution can be funded from sources other than the income the loans actually earned.

"A redemption window is not a redemption."

Now run the money. Put $100,000 into a fund paying a 9.1% distribution rate and the payouts run about $9,100 a year. Now ask for it back. In a quarter where requests run about 10% of shares against a 5% cap, the pro rata fill is about half: roughly $50,000 comes back and the rest waits. At Apollo's third-quarter ratio of 5% filled against 14.7% requested, it is closer to a third, about $34,000. Even Blackstone's own math says asking twice gets you about 75% back in about 90 days. The 9.1% was real the whole time. The money simply was not yours to move on your schedule.

The deeper difference is the starting point. Here is the honest sentence the pitch decks never include: for a patient holder who never needs the money early, a well-run private credit fund can pay its 9% for years, and Goldman's 2% redemption rate shows that investor base exists. For everyone else, and on every line of the risk ledger - the exit, the mark, the payout, what the capital is actually funding - the comparison is not close.

Final Thought

The private credit pitch is the purest example we have covered of a word that sounds reassuring until you ask what it means. Quarterly liquidity is real, but it is a window shared with every other investor, at a price set by a model, five percent at a time. It is not a free lunch, and it is not a door you hold the key to.
