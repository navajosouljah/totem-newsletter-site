Charlie Bilello's 10% Lesson

The cleanest, most data-driven case for long-term equity ownership delivers roughly 10% annualized over nearly a century. Respect the history. Understand the trade-offs. Then ask the six structural questions that separate documented commerce from market expectation.

8 min read Share

The number is 10%. If you follow financial media, you have seen it a thousand times. If you follow Charlie Bilello, you have seen it with the data behind it.

Who he is

Charlie Bilello serves as Chief Market Strategist at Creative Planning, a firm overseeing more than $370 billion in client assets. With nearly 800,000 followers on X and a widely read "The Week in Charts" series, he ranks among the most respected, non-hype voices in financial media. His work appears regularly in Barron's, Bloomberg, and the Wall Street Journal. Bilello focuses on data, charts, and historical context rather than predictions.

He does not sell hope. He shows you what happened.

What he said

Bilello consistently returns to one central statistic:

"The S&P 500 has returned an average of 10% per year since 1928 despite an average intra-year drawdown of -16%."

He emphasizes that there is "no upside without occasional downside, no reward without risk." He also notes the average is more theoretical than practical: the S&P 500 has landed within 2% of that 10% figure in only about 4 of the last 98 years.

His broader thesis is clear and compelling: over the long run, owning productive businesses has beaten lending and cash by a wide margin. $1 put into the S&P 500 in 1928 has compounded into extraordinary wealth through dividends and price appreciation, even after surviving depressions, wars, inflation spikes, and multiple 50%+ crashes.

This is the steel-manned case for equities that every serious allocator must respect.

Where we agree

We agree with Bilello on several core points.

Long-term ownership of productive assets has been the primary engine of wealth creation for those with patience and discipline. Compounding is powerful. Cash and bonds have delivered meaningfully lower returns over the same periods. And volatility is the price of admission for the equity premium.

His data-driven approach cuts through noise. In stretched markets like today - with the Buffett Indicator near 231.7% as of mid-June 2026 - his emphasis on historical context and risk awareness is particularly valuable.

Where it gets complicated

The challenge is not whether 10% annualized is "good." Over decades it has been excellent. The complications appear when you examine the structure beneath the average:

Principal faces full market risk with no defined protection. Distributions - dividends and buybacks - are never known in advance and can be cut. The cycle spans decades, with painful intra-year and multi-year drawdowns. You can choose what to own, but you cannot control outcomes or timing.

Even the strongest historical performer leaves capital exposed to sequences of bad years that can derail plans - especially for those closer to needing liquidity.

Two answers to the same question

The question is fundamentally the same: How do I put capital to work in a way that compounds over time while managing risk?

Bilello's data offers one proven answer: own equities for the long run, accept volatility, and let history do the heavy lifting. It has worked for generations.

Totem offers another: deploy capital into verified, insured commercial transactions tied to Fortune 100 purchase orders. Accept a shorter cycle with contractual fees, full insured principal protection, and zero realized defaults across more than 4,000 insured transactions.

One relies on market expectation. The other funds documented execution.

Neither is where you put everything. But the person who understands both has more options than the person who only knows one.

Closing thought

Ten percent compounded over decades builds real wealth. That is not in dispute. What is worth examining is the path - the drawdowns, the decades of patience required, and the fact that not a single dollar of principal is protected along the way.

Some capital belongs on that path. Some belongs somewhere the fee is written down before you wire the money, the principal is insured before the goods ship, and the cycle ends in 90 days instead of 90 months.

The people who see both options clearly tend to sleep better than the ones who only see one.
