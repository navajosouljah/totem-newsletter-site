Tuesday Briefing • Tuesday, August 11, 2026

WTI Surged 5% Monday to $82.13 as Iran Demands the US Lift Its Naval Blockade Before Hormuz Reopens - Trump Claims Total Control of the Strait, Demands Iran Pay Reparations. Oil Adding Another 2% Tuesday. September Rate Hike Odds Jump to 51%. USDA Crop Progress Held Corn Steady at 61% - Breaking the Four-Week Decline. Bitcoin Falls to $64,000. Wednesday 8:30 AM ET: July CPI and the First Official USDA Corn Yield Estimate Land Against a Sharply Different Oil Backdrop.

Iran says Hormuz stays shut until the US lifts its naval blockade - and oil repriced fast, with WTI up 5% to $82.13 and Brent near $89.80 Tuesday. Wednesday's July CPI and the first official corn yield estimate now land against $82-plus oil and 51% September hike odds.

The Quick Read

The Hormuz deal is not happening soon, and oil knows it. WTI surged 5% Monday - from $78.18 to $82.13 - and Brent is adding another 2.4% Tuesday morning to approximately $89.80, putting WTI near $85.

The move was not a surprise - it was the market's adjustment to new factual information. Iran formally demanded the US lift its naval blockade before any Hormuz opening. Trump rejected that condition and claimed total US control of the waterway. Iran named a hardline Revolutionary Guard commander to its top national security post, and an ADNOC-linked vessel was attacked by an Iranian missile in the strait. None of those developments point toward a near-term deal. The cumulative move from last Thursday's framework announcement (WTI at $75 when the deal seemed close) to Tuesday morning ($85) is approximately 13% in four trading days - not noise, but a systematic repricing of the probability that Hormuz re-opens in August.

The rate implications of this repricing are direct. Oil at $85 WTI is roughly $7 higher than the backdrop against which last week's inflation data was being anticipated. Energy is one of the three channels through which higher commodity prices enter CPI - the others being food (which has its own corn-yield wildcard on Wednesday) and core services (which are less immediately affected). The July CPI reading at 8:30 AM ET Wednesday captures energy prices through approximately early-to-mid July - before the Hormuz situation developed - so Wednesday's print does not yet reflect the oil move. But the September CPI reading will. The three FOMC dissenters who voted for a July hike were already arguing that the rate-hold thesis relied on inflation declining without further commodity shocks. Monday's oil move is exactly the commodity shock they were warning about. September hike probability at 51% on Tuesday morning reflects the market's recognition that the rate-hold narrative is now competing with a new inflation input that was not present last Friday.

Monday's USDA Crop Progress report, released at 4:00 PM ET, provided the week's one piece of positive macro news. Corn held at 61% good-to-excellent - the same reading as August 2 - breaking the four-week consecutive decline that had run from 67% on July 13 to 61% on August 2. The weekly stabilization does not erase the concern about July heat stress during grain fill, but it removes the fifth consecutive decline that would have provided additional validation for the weather premium built into corn futures at $4.39-4.42 per bushel. Whether that premium was correctly sized remains the question Wednesday's official yield estimate answers. A yield at or above 181 bushels per acre - the approximate historical baseline - suggests the crop was more resilient than four consecutive weeks of ratings declines implied. A yield below 179 bu/acre suggests the ratings correctly described real damage. Wednesday's 8:30 AM ET release answers both of Tuesday's open market questions simultaneously: the inflation path (CPI) and the food-price component of that path (corn yield). Both within the same minute.

Crypto's risk-off move - Bitcoin to $64,017 (-1.9%), ETH to $1,876 (-2.6%), XRP to $1.00 (-2.9%) - is the market's acknowledgment that geopolitical escalation competes with the CLARITY Act's September 15 vote for the narrative driver of digital assets. The CLARITY Act's procedural timeline has not changed: cloture was filed August 8, the Senate returns September 14, and the floor vote is scheduled for the week of September 15. But institutional crypto positioning responds to near-term macro risk as well as long-horizon regulatory catalysts. Oil at $82-85 and September hike odds above 50% are conditions under which institutional capital de-risks broadly, and crypto is not insulated from that pattern. XRP at $1.00 is back at its most critical near-term support level - the same level from which it has bounced in each of the prior two tests this summer.

Bottom Line
  • For housing, Monday's oil surge interrupted the most plausible near-term rate improvement scenario. The path from 6.69% to 6.30-6.40% that was contingent on sequential cold CPI readings now requires not just cold July CPI but also a Hormuz resolution that removes the oil price pressure from the August and September inflation readings. Without Hormuz resolution, oil at $84-85 becomes August energy CPI, which arrives September 11, which the September 15-16 FOMC will have in hand when deciding rates. The scenario where the Fed holds in September despite oil at $84-85 is possible - but it requires July CPI to print cold enough to offset the commodity pressure, and the Fed to conclude the oil move is transitory. Neither is the base case. The more probable near-term trajectory for mortgage rates is toward 6.75-6.80% (Thursday PMMS) rather than the 6.60-6.65% that was the pre-Monday expectation.

The S&P 500 near its record (portfolio wealth) remains a positive for asset-holding households. The contrast between those two groups is the week's clearest K-shaped expression.

Gas prices (approximately August 11): approximately $4.01-4.08/gallon not yet reflecting Monday's oil surge due to the 2-to-4 week transmission lag; the prior trajectory toward $3.90-3.95 nationally has reversed; at $84-85 WTI, the trajectory is now toward $4.20-4.30 nationally by mid-September; the states that had reached sub-$4.00 (Indiana $3.49, South Carolina $3.57, Tennessee $3.58) will see those levels return above $4.00 in the coming weeks

Bottom Line
  • The household finance picture on Tuesday splits cleanly along asset ownership. Households with S&P 500 exposure - through 401(k)s, IRAs, or direct holdings - are approximately at the same portfolio level as Friday (S&P 500 at 7,749, down 0.1% from the record 7,757.64). Households without equity market exposure are facing a gas price increase that has not yet appeared at the pump but is mathematically coming: WTI at $84-85 versus $78.18 a week ago is a 7-9% crude increase that, at the normal transmission rate, produces approximately $0.15-0.20 per gallon at the pump within three to four weeks. For the average US vehicle that consumes approximately 40 gallons per month, that is a $6-8 monthly household cost increase. Combined with 22.18% credit card APRs and a 2.7% personal savings rate, the non-asset-owning household has almost no financial buffer for a cost increase of any magnitude.

Around the World

Asian markets are mixed Tuesday morning as oil's continued surge creates divergent outcomes: energy-exporting economies benefit while energy-importing economies face a cost shock. European markets face the most direct headwind from $89-90 Brent. The Hormuz situation's direct impact on global shipping lanes elevates the Japan and Korea risk more than any macro data release could.

Asian markets (Tuesday morning): mixed; energy-importing economies (Japan, Korea, most of Southeast Asia) face rising input costs from oil at $84-85; energy-exporting economies benefit; Japanese markets remain near record Nikkei levels but face a technology headwind from the semiconductor sector's ongoing softening and a household purchasing power headwind from higher oil imports; Korea faces continued semiconductor sector uncertainty combined with oil-driven cost pressure on its heavy industrial sector
Japan / Nikkei 225: approximately 65,606 range; Japan is among the most oil-import-dependent economies in the developed world - approximately 90% of Japan's oil is imported; the move from $78 to $85 WTI in four sessions represents approximately $500-600 million in additional annual oil import costs per day at the margin; Bank of Japan's position on rate policy is already complex given domestic inflation considerations; an external oil shock at $84-85 adds inflationary pressure without boosting domestic demand
European markets: Stoxx 600 under modest pressure from $87-90 Brent; Europe had been one of the primary beneficiaries of oil's mid-July decline from $93 to $75-76, which reduced energy costs for manufacturing-heavy economies; the reversal from $75-76 back to $89-90 Brent erases most of that benefit; European energy-intensive manufacturing - steel, chemicals, automotive - faces renewed cost pressure that will appear in earnings over the next two quarters
China: China's July export surge ($397.85 billion, +23.9% YoY) remains the constructive trade context; but China is also the world's largest oil importer, and the move from $78 to $85+ WTI is a direct cost hit to Chinese manufacturing margins; China's 49.2% manufacturing PMI (technically in contraction) was already under pressure from domestic demand weakness; higher input costs from oil add a second margin constraint to a sector already running below-trend
Strait of Hormuz direct exposure: approximately 20-21 million barrels per day of crude oil and products transit the Strait of Hormuz in normal conditions, representing approximately 20% of global petroleum liquids trade; the US naval blockade and Iranian counter-measures have disrupted this flow; partial transit continues but at reduced volumes and higher insurance premiums; the economic impact of sustained Hormuz restriction falls most heavily on Asian importers (Japan, Korea, China, India) and European refinery systems that depend on Middle Eastern crude
Bottom Line
  • The Hormuz situation's global economic impact has a specific geography: it falls most heavily on the economies that are farthest from the US domestic energy system. The US, with its domestic shale production near record levels and LNG export capacity that bypasses Hormuz entirely, is the least directly exposed major economy to Hormuz disruption. Japan, Korea, and China - which import the majority of their oil through Hormuz - are the most directly exposed. The global oil price increase (WTI +12-13% in four sessions) redistributes income from oil-importing to oil-exporting economies at a scale that matters for quarterly GDP readings. The International Monetary Fund's 2026 growth forecasts were written before this week's Hormuz escalation; the downside revisions for Japan, Korea, and China from sustained $84-90 oil are now a live forecast risk for Q3 and Q4 2026.
