The Maturity Question: Oil and Gas Notes Paying Up to 16.5% a Year, and What It Takes to Get Your Money Back

Phoenix Energy One sells notes to the public at 6.00% to 11.00% a year and private bonds to accredited buyers at 13.0% to 16.5%, with terms that run as long as eleven years. Drilling partnerships and royalty trusts make a different promise. We read the filings and ask one question of each: when, and how, do you get your money back?

10–15 minute read Share

Every issue, we analyze the published strategies of prominent voices in the wealth-building space. Where we agree, we say so. Where we differ, we run both through our six-question Totem Scorecard and publish the comparison. This week we take on a pitch our members hear often: put your money into oil, gas or minerals, collect a double-digit rate, and wait.

This Week's Pitch

The version we hear most goes like this: a company takes your money, pays about 13% a year, and you leave it in for about 13 years. The name attached to it most often is Phoenix Energy, the Irvine, California oil and gas company formerly called Phoenix Capital Group Holdings. It changed its name to Phoenix Energy One on January 23, 2025, according to its own SEC prospectus.

Phoenix is a real operating business. Its filings report revenue of $687.2 million in 2025 and $704.5 million in the first six months of 2026, 6,612 mineral and royalty acquisitions since 2019, and its own drilling arm with 173 gross wells drilled as of June 30, 2026. It pays for that growth largely by selling debt to individuals, in several programs at once.

The challenged piece: the oil and gas income-note pitch - a stated double-digit rate in exchange for money committed for many years to a company that is borrowing to drill. We checked the 13% and the 13 years against the filings. One is close. One is not.

The Energy Income Playbook

Strip away the brochures and most versions run the same five steps:

  1. Point to something real. Wells, acreage, barrels and royalty checks. All of it exists.
  2. Quote a rate. A number well above a bank account, paid monthly or added to the balance.
  3. Pick a term. Longer terms pay more. At Phoenix, the proposed Access Notes run from 7.5% for two years to 11.0% for eleven.
  4. Describe the exit. Hold to maturity, or ask for early redemption on the issuer's terms.
  5. Put the risks in the filing. They are there, in plain language, for anyone who reads to page 29.

Inside the Programs

Phoenix, the public notes. On July 7, 2026, the SEC declared effective Phoenix's registration for up to $100 million of Phoenix Flex Junior Secured Notes. They pay 6.00% to 7.00% a year, carry a ten-year stated maturity, and let a holder ask for full redemption at set dates every three to eighteen months, with 30 to 45 days' notice. Phoenix had sold about $22.5 million of them by September 30. On October 22, 2026, Phoenix filed a new registration for up to $500 million of Phoenix Access Notes at 7.5% for two years up to 11.0% for eleven years, with a $5,000 minimum. That filing is not yet effective.

Phoenix, the private bonds. This is where the 13% lives. Phoenix's subsidiary Adamantium Capital sells bonds under Rule 506(c), open to accredited buyers only, at 13.0% to 16.5% a year with terms of five to eleven years, per Phoenix's June 30, 2026 quarterly report. Separately, its August 2023 506(c) bonds pay 9.0% to 14.0% with terms of one to eleven years. Its earlier Regulation A bonds, open to the general public, paid 9.0% with three-year terms.

What sits ahead of the holder. Phoenix's October 22 prospectus states that, after the new offering, it would have had about $2,320.8 million of debt as of June 30, 2026, including $848.5 million ranking ahead of the new notes, of which $525.0 million is a senior secured lender, Fortress Credit Corp. It reported a net loss of $34.1 million for the first half of 2026, has restated earlier financial statements, and on October 21, 2026 obtained a waiver of a lender covenant for April through December 2026. The prospectus also says Phoenix may use new note money to pay interest and principal on existing debt.

Getting out early. On the Access Notes, a holder may ask to redeem before maturity at 95% of principal, and all such early redemptions are capped at 10% of the notes outstanding each year. The notes are not listed on any exchange and can be transferred only with Phoenix's consent. In the prospectus's own words, "you must be prepared to hold your Notes to maturity."

The Rest of the Field

Drilling partnerships. Mewbourne Energy Partners 26-A, a Texas-sponsored drilling partnership, filed a Form D on June 18, 2026 for a $150 million private sale of partnership units under Rule 506(b), with a $20,000 minimum and $38.76 million sold to 186 buyers at the time. There is no stated rate: the partner owns a share of wells and is paid from what they produce. Mewbourne's own registered prospectus for an earlier program warned that drilling "includes the possibility of a total loss," that general partners may face unlimited personal liability, and that the interests are illiquid with no market for them.

Listed mineral owners. Black Stone Minerals (NYSE: BSM) owns mineral and royalty interests and trades on an exchange every business day. On October 21, 2026 it declared $0.35 per unit for the third quarter, up about 17% from $0.30 in the first quarter. That is the best case for minerals: real cash, daily liquidity. The trade-off is that the distribution is set by the board each quarter and the unit price moves with the market, so neither is a promise.

Royalty trusts. Permian Basin Royalty Trust (NYSE: PBT) passes royalty income straight through to holders each month. Its October 20, 2026 distribution was $0.045191 per unit, and it included nothing from its largest property, Waddell Ranch, because production costs exceeded gross proceeds. Those excess costs, plus interest, must be recovered before Waddell Ranch pays the trust again. The trustee wrote that it is "difficult to predict what effect these conditions will have on future distributions."

Where We Second the Energy Programs

There is plenty here we will defend, and our members in the field will recognize it.

The assets are real. Phoenix reports more than 9.9 million barrels of oil equivalent produced in 2025. Black Stone and Permian Basin own mineral and royalty interests that have paid holders for decades. Nobody is selling air.

The disclosure is serious. Phoenix files annual and quarterly reports with the SEC and lays out its debt, its losses, its restatement and its covenant waivers in its own prospectus. The listed trusts and partnerships report production, prices and costs month by month. Anyone who reads can know what they own.

Longer money earns more. Paying 11.0% for eleven years and 7.5% for two is an honest price for time and risk. The Flex Notes, with redemption dates as often as every three months, show Phoenix offering shorter exits at lower rates.

Liquidity exists if you want it. BSM and PBT can be sold on any trading day. For a holder who wants mineral exposure and can live with price swings, that is a real advantage, and it wins a round below.

Where We Challenge: The Maturity Question

Our singular point of divergence is the word that decides when you are paid back: maturity.

A stated rate tells you what a borrower intends to pay. Maturity tells you how long your money depends on that borrower staying healthy. At eleven years, a holder is lending through several oil price cycles, several refinancings and whatever the company decides to drill next. Phoenix says it will need about $669.8 million in additional capital through the end of 2028 to fund development, and that it may use new note proceeds to pay existing debt. Those are honest disclosures. They are also the reason the rate is high.

The exits narrow the further you go. The Access Notes return 95 cents on the dollar early, and only while total early redemptions stay under 10% of the notes a year. The drilling partnership has no exit at all. The listed royalty names have a fast exit, but at whatever price the market pays that day, and the payout itself can stop, as Waddell Ranch shows.

"A high rate tells you what a borrower hopes to pay. The maturity date tells you how long you are hoping with them."

Now run the money. Put $100,000 into an eleven-year Access Note at 11.0%, cash interest. If every payment arrives, that is about $11,000 a year, and the $100,000 comes back in 2037. Need it back in year three? The early-redemption price is 95% of principal, or $95,000, and only if the 10% annual cap has room. That is arithmetic from the prospectus terms, not a prediction of ours.

For everyone else, ask the maturity question first.

Final Thought

Oil, gas and minerals are real businesses run by people who know their work, and some of them are reading this. The programs that fund them are honest about their terms when you read the filings: 13.0% to 16.5% for accredited buyers who commit for five to eleven years, 7.5% to 11.0% for the public on terms up to eleven years, and royalty checks that rise and fall with the wells. The question is not whether the asset is real. It is how long your money has to wait, and who is paid before you.
