Weekly Briefing • Saturday, August 1, 2026

Amazon Closes Up 15% at a Record as AWS Confirms the AI Revenue Thesis. Apple Falls ~9% on Guidance - ~$390B in Market Cap Gone. S&P 500 Ends July Down 0.79% Despite a 5-Day Late-Month Rally. Iran Conflict Enters Day 15 - Brent at ~$92. Nonfarm Payrolls August 7 Is Now the Key Test for September.

The Quick Read

The week closes with a clean split that the market has been building toward all month. Amazon confirmed the AI infrastructure trade on the revenue side - up 15% on Friday, closing what appears to be a record session on the back of AWS at 37% year-over-year growth. Apple confirmed the hardware-margin stress side - down approximately 9.17% as Q4 guidance missed consensus and management cited supply constraints and rising memory costs explicitly. The two-tier AI framework that opened the week - cloud platforms that monetize AI demand versus consumer hardware players that pay higher prices for AI components - survived four earnings reports undefeated. Microsoft and Amazon are on one side. Meta and Apple are on the other. The market has now priced both sides with a degree of conviction that a single-quarter reversal is unlikely to undo.

July is now closed at -0.79% for the S&P 500. That number requires both respect and skepticism. Respect, because the index absorbed a chip rout that erased 22% from the KOSPI in a single month, a hawkish FOMC with three dissents for a rate hike, US-Iran strikes resuming after a four-day pause that had briefly built an oil-disinflation argument, a Meta earnings collapse, and an Apple guidance miss - and ended the month nearly flat. Skepticism, because the -0.79% result is an artifact of calendar timing. Microsoft and Amazon both reported in the final three sessions of July. If the earnings sequence had been reversed - the AI wins in the first three days and the FOMC and Iran escalation in the last three - July would have ended down 6% or 7%, not down less than 1%. The S&P 500's resilience is real. So is its concentration risk in the handful of names whose results can move the index by 1-2% in a single session.

The August agenda opens immediately. ISM Manufacturing comes Monday morning - the first read on whether goods production is responding to the slower Q2 GDP growth or holding near the 50-point neutral line. The USDA releases its August crop progress report Monday at 4 PM ET, the most important agriculture data point of the month given the drought conditions in the western Corn Belt that have pushed corn's good-to-excellent rating down to 63%. And the event that dominates the first week of August: July nonfarm payrolls on Friday August 7 at 8:30 AM ET. June came in at 57,000 jobs, well below the 110,000 consensus and the lowest monthly reading in four months. July's number will either confirm that softening trend - creating a potential release valve for the ~81% September hike probability that currently sits in the market - or reverse it, handing the three FOMC dissenters the data confirmation they voted expecting.

The full first week of August is one of the densest data calendars of the year. JOLTS on Tuesday, ADP and ISM Services on Wednesday, jobless claims Thursday, payrolls Friday. Between those five releases, the market will receive more information about the labor market and services sector than it has had since before the July FOMC. The September 15 meeting is 44 days away. The data that will determine whether three dissenters become five - or whether the committee settles back toward a consensus hold - arrives largely this week.

  • Amazon's 15% Friday close is the capstone of a week that tested the AI infrastructure thesis at the highest level and found it intact on the revenue side.   • The S&P 500's -0.79% monthly performance for July is the cleanest illustration of how concentrated the US equity market has become in the names that define its direction. The index absorbed a 22% Korean chip-market rout, a hawkish FOMC surprise, an Iranian missile strike on a US military base, three consecutive oil-above-$90 weeks, a Meta earnings miss, and an Apple guidance shortfall - and the net monthly result is a loss of less than one percent. The mechanism is not mysterious: Microsoft, Amazon, and Alphabet between them generated enough market cap recovery in the final three days of the month to offset most of the prior three weeks' damage. That is not resilience in the broad-market sense. It is concentration expressed as apparent resilience.
  • The Russell 2000's -0.50% close on Friday - the only major index to finish in the red on a day when Nasdaq gained 1% - is the domestic-equity signal most relevant to the August rate debate. Small-cap stocks are more rate-sensitive than large-cap technology because they carry more floating-rate debt, have thinner margins to absorb higher financing costs, and have less pricing power against input cost inflation. The Russell closing lower reflects the ~81% September hike probability sitting in the bond market. If that probability rises above 85-90% on a strong August 7 jobs report, the Russell's relative underperformance will deepen. If it drops to 55-60% on a second soft jobs print, small caps have the most to recover.

The bond market absorbed a positive Amazon earnings print, a soft Q2 GDP number, a modestly disinflationary PCE read, and a dollar that firmed slightly Friday - and held yields near the levels that the hawkish FOMC established on July 29. The persistence of 4.74% through the week's positive macro and earnings data tells you the bond market is treating the three-dissenter committee distribution as a structurally more important signal than any individual data print.

  • The 10-year Treasury ending July at 4.74% is the rate story of the month summarized in a single number. The 10-year began July around 4.40-4.50% on market expectation of a September cut. It ends July at 4.74% after the FOMC repriced September from a cut to a likely hike. That 25-30 basis point shift is the single most consequential financial market event of July measured by downstream effects: mortgage rates up to 6.66%, small-cap stocks underperforming, housing affordability further deteriorating, variable-rate borrowers facing higher costs. The softer GDP and PCE data Thursday - which would ordinarily push the 10-year lower - barely moved the needle because the bond market has concluded that the question for September is not whether the macro data is soft enough to cut, but whether it is soft enough to hold against three FOMC members who have already publicly committed to a hike.
  • The path from ~81% September hike probability to either 90%+ or 55% runs through the August 7 payrolls report and the August CPI (released September 11). June's 57,000 job reading was anomalously low - below the four-month trailing average of approximately 90,000. If July rebounds to 100,000-plus, the three FOMC dissenters have the labor market confirmation they were looking for at the July meeting. If July produces another 50,000-70,000 reading, the argument for holding becomes harder for even the most hawkish committee members to dismiss. The honest assessment is that one payrolls print is rarely enough to move already-committed FOMC dissenting votes. But if July comes in below 75,000, Chair Warsh faces a more difficult press conference in September than he did in July. He has stated publicly the Fed will not hesitate to stop inflation. A sub-75,000 jobs print with core PCE still at 3.3% would test that formulation directly.

Savings & CD Rates

The rate environment that had briefly looked like it was on the cusp of shifting toward cuts is now firmly in hold-or-hike territory. The CD decision calculus that changed on July 29 has not changed back. Savers who locked in 1-year CDs at 4.50% before the FOMC meeting are in a demonstrably better position than they expected one week ago.

Best high-yield savings: ~4.15% APY (leading online banks)
Bottom Line
  • The CD question has simplified since July 29. Before the FOMC, the choice between a 4.50% 1-year CD and a 4.15% high-yield savings account was framed as "lock in before cuts arrive versus stay flexible." That framing is now inverted. With September hike probability at ~81%, the case for a savings account's flexibility is that rates may rise further, not that cuts are coming. The savings account wins if September brings a hike and rates subsequently increase to 4.50-4.75% on new offers. The CD wins if rates hold at current levels through mid-2027 and the savings account's rate eventually drifts lower as other banks compete down. Neither scenario is a losing position for the saver. Both are materially better than where savers were in a zero-rate world. The relevant frame for August has shifted from "when do cuts come" to "when do hikes stop."

  • August opens with housing in the same position it has been in all summer, only more so. Mortgage rates are at 6.66% and rising, not falling. The buyer who deferred a purchase in June expecting September rate cuts now faces a September hike probability of ~81% and a mortgage rate that has moved 16 basis points in the wrong direction in the past week alone. At 6.66% on a median home price of $440,600 with a 20% down payment, the monthly principal-and-interest payment is approximately $2,440 - a level that price-to-income ratios suggest is beyond reach for a median household in most of the country's major metropolitan areas. The market is not locked; it is fractured. The upper tier - move-up buyers with equity from prior purchases and high-income professionals with stable employment - is transacting. The entry-level and mid-tier buyer - first-time purchasers, FHA borrowers, households who stretched to buy at 2021-2022 prices on adjustable rates - is increasingly under stress. Both descriptions are true simultaneously. The median price being at a record does not contradict 35% of sellers cutting prices.   • The H1 foreclosure acceleration - 227,548 filings, +21% year-over-year, across 77% of US metros - is the housing market's most underreported story and its most consequential supply signal. These filings represent households that acquired their properties at lower rates, on flexible terms, or with thin equity buffers that have been exhausted by a combination of price appreciation that did not translate into personal financial gains, payment resets on adjustable mortgages, or income disruptions in a cooling labor market. At 6.66% rates, fewer qualified buyers exist to absorb the distressed supply as it flows through the foreclosure process. The net effect is downward pressure on the lower and middle segments of the price distribution - which the aggregate median does not capture - and an increasing divergence between the housing market that shows up in national statistics and the housing market that a first-time buyer encounters.

Bottom Line
  • The household sector enters August with its buffers materially thinner than at any point since the pandemic reopening. The personal savings rate at 2.7% means households are saving less than three cents per dollar earned. The credit card severe delinquency rate at 13.1% means roughly one in eight credit card dollars is in deep arrears. 47% of BNPL users are paying late, and 29% are using buy-now-pay-later services for grocery purchases - a metric that speaks to structural cash flow stress rather than discretionary financing behavior. Together, these indicators describe a consumer sector that has maintained aggregate spending through a combination of savings drawdown, credit expansion, and BNPL deferral. All three of those mechanisms become more expensive and harder to access if September brings a rate hike. The potential August stress cascade - oil above $90 at the pump, a September rate hike that raises variable borrowing costs, and a jobs market that may continue softening - lands on a household sector with substantially less cushion to absorb it than existed at the start of 2026.
  • The divergence between aggregate consumer spending data and household financial stress metrics is the central tension in reading the US economy heading into August. The PCE spending number looks resilient. The delinquency data, savings rate, and BNPL metrics look stressed. Both are accurate descriptions of different parts of the same population. The K-shaped consumer recovery - high-income households spending freely, protected by equity-market wealth and stable employment - is generating the aggregate spending figures. Lower-income and middle-income households - squeezed by higher gas prices, credit card rates above 23%, and savings buffers near zero - are generating the delinquency figures. A September rate hike does not affect both halves of that K symmetrically. The high-income household with substantial fixed-rate assets is largely insulated. The household carrying a variable-rate balance at 23.8% APR is directly and immediately exposed to any rate increase.

Bottom Line
  • Bitcoin at $62,865 heading into August reflects the precise position crypto occupies in the current macro environment: sensitive enough to risk sentiment to rise on Amazon's earnings-driven euphoria, but anchored enough to the rate environment to give most of it back when the 10-year holds at 4.74% and the dollar firms. The path to a sustained BTC move above $65,000 into new territory requires either a meaningful reduction in September hike probability - which would need a sequence of soft macro data starting with August 7 payrolls - or a resolution of the Iran conflict that removes the geopolitical safe-haven bid from the dollar and pushes risk appetite into growth assets broadly. Neither condition is imminent. The base case for August is BTC trading in a $60,000-$67,000 range as the market waits for the jobs and CPI data that will determine September's direction. Range-bound crypto in an ~81% hike environment is not a bearish outcome; it is a neutral one in a macro context that could easily be worse.

Around the World

July ends with a historic session in Seoul bookending a historic month of losses. The KOSPI's 17.91% gain on Friday - confirmed as the largest single-session rise in the index's history - did not fully reverse July's 22% monthly decline. The net July performance is still the KOSPI's worst month since the 1997 Asian financial crisis. Europe faces continued oil-cost pressure and a dollar at 102-103 that creates translation headwinds for exporters. Japan's semiconductor-equipment names and tech ecosystem stocks rallied alongside the KOSPI recovery.

South Korea (KOSPI - confirmed Friday close): 6,595.45 +17.91%; largest single-day gain in index history; SK Hynix at 30% daily upper limit; Samsung +27%; July monthly performance: approximately -22%, worst since 1997 despite the record recovery session
Japan (Nikkei 225 - confirmed Friday close): ~64,362 +4.03%; semiconductor-equipment and tech names rallied on KOSPI recovery and Amazon AWS data; July Nikkei monthly performance was positive, cushioned by yen weakness and AI supply chain exposure
Europe: Brent above $92 adds renewed energy cost pressure for oil-importing industries; dollar at 102-103 creates translation headwinds for European exporters; ECB watching US rate divergence as Fed leans hawkish while European growth continues to slow; no resolution path on US-Iran conflict that would relieve European energy costs
China: tech sector cautiously positive on AI demand signal from AWS; real estate sector stress ongoing; domestic stimulus measures not yet sufficient to reverse property market deflation; Hang Seng moderately positive on the week
Dollar index (DXY): ~102-103 firmed Friday after modest Thursday softening; rate differential with rest of world remains wide given ~81% September hike probability versus most other central banks on hold or easing; strong dollar is a universal headwind for commodity-importing emerging markets
Bottom Line
  • The KOSPI's July story - 22% down for the month, 18% up in a single session, net still approximately 6% below where July began - is the purest expression of forward-fear dynamics in 2026. The index did not decline 22% because Samsung's factories stopped producing chips. It did not decline 22% because SK Hynix's HBM3 orders evaporated. It declined 22% because the market concluded - based on Meta's EPS miss, Nvidia's $250B guarantee uncertainty, and the Fed's hawkish surprise - that AI demand was not converting into sustainable revenue at the hyperscaler level. Amazon's AWS at 37% growth disproved that conclusion in a single data point on Thursday evening, and the Korean market repriced 18% of the fear premium in a single Friday session. The remaining gap - July still ended approximately -6% on net - reflects two things: the market appropriately pricing some of the fear forward, since Amazon confirming AI demand does not automatically resolve the FOMC dissent or the Iran conflict or the mortgage rate trajectory; and the mechanical reality that a 22% decline requires a 28% recovery to be flat, and 18% is not 28%.
  • Japan's 4% Friday gain and relatively positive July monthly performance tells the structural story of the AI supply chain more clearly than Korea's volatile month does. Japan's semiconductor equipment companies - Tokyo Electron, Advantest, and their peers - benefit from AI demand one step removed from the direct memory and chip producers. When AI demand is confirmed at the hyperscaler level, the equipment companies that supply the factories that produce the AI chips get the pull-through demand signal without the same degree of direct fear exposure that Korean foundry and memory stocks carry. The Nikkei's relative stability through July - compared to the KOSPI's 22% decline - reflects this structural position in the supply chain. It is a less volatile way to hold AI exposure, but it is also a less leveraged one in either direction.

August opens with the densest economic data week of the quarter. Five major releases in five days will provide the first post-FOMC read on labor markets, services activity, and manufacturing. The week culminates with the July nonfarm payrolls on Friday August 7 - the number that will define the September rate debate more than any other single data point.

July's read will indicate whether goods production is maintaining expansion momentum or beginning to cool. Followed by USDA Weekly Crop Progress report at 4 PM ET - the most important agriculture release of the summer. Current baseline: corn 63% good-to-excellent, drought stress in western Corn Belt.
Aug 4 (Tue) - JOLTS Job Openings for June (BLS, 10 AM ET). Labor market tightness data; the three FOMC dissenters cited labor market conditions as part of their hike case. A significant decline in openings would soften that argument.
Aug 5 (Wed) - ADP National Employment Report for July (8:15 AM ET). Private-sector payrolls preview; June ADP was soft. Also ISM Services PMI for July (10 AM ET). Services activity has been the resilient corner of the economy; any crack in services would shift the September calculus.
Aug 6 (Thu) - Weekly jobless claims (8:30 AM ET). Also Freddie Mac weekly mortgage rate survey releases Thursday - the first read on whether 10-year at 4.74% has pushed 30-year mortgage rates further above 6.66%.
Aug 7 (Fri) - July nonfarm payrolls (BLS, 8:30 AM ET). The key number. June was 57,000 - well below the 110,000 consensus and the lowest in four months. A strong July rebound pushes September hike probability above 85-90%. A second consecutive sub-75,000 reading creates a credible hold argument even for the three FOMC dissenters. Unemployment rate and average hourly earnings will accompany the headline figure.
Sep 11 (Thu) - August CPI: the data point that retroactively determines the September FOMC debate. First comprehensive read on July energy (Brent above $90 for most of the month), tariff pass-through, crop-weather effects on food prices, and services inflation. Four days before the FOMC.
Sep 15-16 (Mon-Tue) - Next FOMC meeting. September hike probability currently at approximately 81%. First meeting with new Summary of Economic Projections (dot plot) since March. The dot plot will show whether the three dissenters' hike view has gained or lost additional committee support since July.
