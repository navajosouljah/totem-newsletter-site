Friday Briefing • Friday, August 7, 2026

Jobless Claims Fall to 199,000 - Third Consecutive Week Below 200K, Longest Such Streak Since 1969 - Directly Contradicting ADP's 44,000 Miss. Oil Rebounds 3.4% to $77.75 on Iran-Oman Hormuz Route Agreement. Dow Snaps Three-Day Record Streak, Down 464. Challenger Cuts at Two-Year Low. Freddie Mac 30-Year Rises to 6.69%. BLS July Jobs Report at 8:30 AM ET This Morning.

Thursday's initial jobless claims at 199,000 - the third consecutive week below 200,000 and the longest such streak since 1969 - directly contradicted Wednesday's ADP print of 44,000 and partially restored the September rate hike case. The two readings describe the same labor market from opposite sides: layoffs are historically rare while new hiring has slowed sharply. Oil reversed Wednesday's deal-optimism decline, rebounding 3.4% to approximately $77.75 as Iran and Oman agreed in principle on Hormuz shipping lane coordinates - a framework, not yet a signed deal. Higher crude weighed on the cyclical names that had driven the Dow's three-day record run, snapping the streak with a 464-point decline. Treasury yields rose on the strong claims data and oil recovery. Freddie Mac's 30-year mortgage moved to 6.69%, reversing the direction that analysts had anticipated from Wednesday's disinflation trade. The BLS July jobs report releases at 8:30 AM ET this morning - the tiebreaker between Thursday's resilient claims and Wednesday's historically weak ADP.

The Quick Read

What Thursday settled - and what it didn't: Initial jobless claims for the week ending August 1 came in at 199,000 - up just 1,000 from the prior week's revised 198,000, below the 205,000 consensus forecast, and below 200,000 for the third consecutive week. The four-week moving average fell to 198,750. That is the lowest sustained level in this metric since 1969, when the US workforce was roughly half its current size. The reading is a direct labor market data point, and it says the same thing every week it prints below 200,000: employers are not letting workers go. Whatever caused ADP to report 44,000 new private sector jobs in July - the weakest reading in six months - it was not a wave of layoffs. The two readings are measuring different things. ADP measures gross new hiring. Claims measure gross layoffs. They can diverge when employers simultaneously slow new hiring and slow layoffs. That is the most coherent explanation for this week's labor data: the US labor market is in a freeze, not a freefall.

Oil reversed course sharply on Thursday. WTI crude rebounded approximately 3.4% from Wednesday's close near $75 to approximately $77.75 per barrel, driven by news that Iran and Oman have reached agreement in principle on the geographic coordinates for a Hormuz shipping lane. The phrase "agreed in principle" is doing substantial work in that sentence. As of Thursday, no deal had been formally signed, no shipping lanes had been reopened, and the US-Iran gap over sovereignty and toll authority remained open. Treasury Secretary Bessent's "today or tomorrow" language from Wednesday has not yet produced a signed agreement. The market treated the framework agreement as a constructive signal - but not enough to add to Wednesday's 7% crude decline. WTI at $77.75 is approximately $5 above Wednesday's close and approximately $15 below its mid-July peak of approximately $93. The directional story on crude is still lower if the deal is consummated. The risk is that it isn't.

The Dow snapped its three-day record streak, falling 464 points to 53,885. Higher oil prices directly reversed the dynamic that had driven Wednesday's Dow record: the cyclical and industrial names that benefit from lower crude were sold as oil rebounded. The S&P 500 fell 0.18% to 7,709.96. The Nasdaq declined just 0.06% to 26,348.35, a smaller loss than the Dow because semiconductor names, which had led the Nasdaq's weakness earlier in the week via AppLovin and the Korean chip sell-off, did not extend their declines on Thursday. The Russell 2000 fell 0.58% to 3,001.55, giving back a portion of Wednesday's 1.85% gain - rate-sensitive small-caps sold off modestly as Treasury yields rose on the strong claims data.

Freddie Mac released its Primary Mortgage Market Survey Thursday, showing the 30-year fixed rate moved to 6.69% - up 3 basis points from the prior week's 6.66%. The direction surprised the market. Wednesday's oil-driven disinflation trade had pushed the 10-year Treasury toward 4.63%, and analysts had anticipated a modest decline in the Freddie Mac reading. Instead, the combined effect of Thursday's strong claims data and the oil rebound pushed the 10-year back to approximately 4.668% - and the Freddie Mac survey, which captures the prior week's lending environment, reflected a market that had not yet fully adjusted to the August 5 ADP miss. The net result: mortgage rates went up, not down, in the first official weekly reading following the Hormuz deal optimism.

This morning at 8:30 AM ET, the BLS July jobs report releases. Economists are forecasting approximately 83,000-87,500 nonfarm payrolls and an unemployment rate holding at 4.2%. The ADP reading of 44,000 has moved the whisper number well below consensus. The initial claims reading of 199,000 has partially restored it. The BLS number is the tiebreaker - and it is the single most consequential data point for the Fed's September 15-16 decision between the July 29 hold and the next meeting. A BLS print above 100,000 restores the September hike probability toward 55-60%. A print below 50,000 - consistent with the ADP signal - likely removes September from the base case. The range between 50,000 and 100,000 leaves the September decision genuinely two-sided, with the three FOMC dissenters and Chair Warsh's majority continuing to hold irreconcilable interpretations of the same data.

Friday Morning

  • Initial jobless claims (week ending August 1): 199,000 - third consecutive week below 200K, longest streak since 1969. Claims rose just 1,000 from the prior week's revised 198,000 and came in well below the 205,000 consensus forecast. The claims reading lands in direct opposition to Wednesday's ADP print. The correct interpretation is not that one is right and the other wrong: layoffs at historic lows (claims) and new hiring at a six-month low (ADP) describe a labor market in stasis - employers are neither letting people go nor bringing new workers on at the rate of the prior two years.
  • Challenger, Gray & Christmas job cuts (July 2026): 33,429 - lowest monthly total in two years. July's announced corporate layoff total was down 27% from June's 45,849 and down 46% from July 2025's 62,075. The Challenger data reinforces the claims picture: the economy is not in a layoff cycle. It is in a hiring pause, concentrated in the sectors where AI is displacing roles that historically required human headcount.

The US position, stated by Treasury Secretary Bessent and the White House, is that any deal cannot allow Iran to charge transit tolls or claim sovereignty over shipping lanes. Iran's foreign ministry said the framework "is not subject to US interference." The gap between "agreed in principle between Iran and Oman" and "operationally reopened with US approval" remains significant. Oil markets treated Thursday's news as constructive but not transformative: WTI moved from $75 to $77.75, recovering about half of Wednesday's deal-optimism decline. If the deal is formally signed and lanes reopen, WTI has a plausible path toward $70-72. If negotiations collapse, oil likely returns toward $82-85.

  • Freddie Mac 30-year mortgage rises to 6.69%, reversing expected post-Hormuz direction. Analysts had anticipated a modest decline in the 30-year reading following Wednesday's bond market rally on disinflation expectations. Instead, the strong claims data and oil recovery pushed yields higher during the survey period, and the Freddie Mac reading captured that shift. A 30-year rate at 6.69% - up, not down - is not what the housing market needed heading into fall buying season.

Bottom Line
  • The week has produced three separate labor market signals, all pointing different directions. ADP said 44,000 - hiring stopped. ISM Services employment contracted to 47.4% - service businesses are cutting headcount. Initial claims printed at 199,000 for the third consecutive week - no one is getting fired. The economic scenario that makes all three readings simultaneously true is a labor market freeze: employers who got burned in 2021-2022 by over-hiring and then had to lay off are now managing headcount conservatively - not adding, not cutting. That is a labor market that is "healthy" by the metrics that capture layoffs and "weak" by the metrics that capture new hiring. The Fed's three dissenters built their September hike case on a tight labor market. The data this week says the labor market is tight in the sense of low unemployment and low layoffs, but not tight in the sense of sustained new hiring pressure. That distinction matters enormously for whether wage inflation re-accelerates.
  • The Freddie Mac 30-year mortgage at 6.69% is the week's clearest illustration of how much depends on this morning's BLS number. A 6.69% rate was priced when the market was uncertain whether Wednesday's ADP miss or Thursday's claims strength was the real signal. A BLS number above 100,000 - confirming claims over ADP - likely pushes mortgage rates toward 6.75-6.85% by next Thursday, as September hike probability moves above 60%. A BLS number below 50,000 - confirming ADP over claims - likely pulls mortgage rates back toward 6.50-6.60% as the September hold becomes the base case and the bond market begins pricing the first cut. The 20-30 basis point range between those outcomes represents approximately $45-70 per month on a $400,000 mortgage - a meaningful real-money consequence of a single employment report.

Bottom Line
  • Thursday's oil rebound to $77.75 is not a reversal of the Hormuz deal thesis - it is the market's recalibration to where the deal actually stands. Wednesday priced a signed deal. Thursday priced a framework. The distance between those two states is larger than the market assumed on Wednesday afternoon when Bessent said "today or tomorrow." The US-Iran sovereignty dispute over whether Iran can charge transit fees for ships using the Hormuz corridor is not a drafting detail. It is a fundamental question about who controls the strait. Iran's position - that the framework is between Iran and Oman and is not subject to US interference - is incompatible with the US position that no deal can include Iranian toll authority. Those two positions have to be reconciled or the deal fails. Oil at $77.75 is pricing a roughly 50/50 probability that the reconciliation happens. A signed deal takes WTI toward $70-72 within days. A breakdown takes it back above $82-83.
  • Gas at $4.063 nationally is moving in the right direction for household budgets, but the 2-to-4 week lag between crude price movements and pump prices means Thursday's oil rebound will show up at the pump in mid-to-late August. For Gulf Coast consumers who were approaching $3.50 earlier this week on the WTI drop toward $75, Thursday's rebound to $77.75 removes roughly $0.08-0.12 of the anticipated pump-price decline. For California drivers who were not going to see pump relief anyway, the crude move in either direction matters less than the refinery capacity constraints that keep California prices structurally above the national average regardless of WTI levels.

The scenario that is bearish - a clean "risk-on" environment where rates rise sharply and geopolitical risk premia compress simultaneously - is not the current environment.

Challenger job cuts (July 2026): 33,429 lowest in two years; AI cited as top reason for 5th consecutive month; the pattern - AI eliminations concentrated in white-collar roles while overall layoffs are historically low - is creating a K-shaped labor market within the knowledge economy itself: AI-adjacent roles are gaining and AI-replaceable roles are under structural pressure
PCE inflation (June, Commerce Dept.): -0.1% MoM +3.7% YoY; July CPI releases August 12; the June PCE deflation is the most recent read on whether household purchasing power is improving
Core PCE (ex-food & energy, June): +0.1% MoM +3.3% YoY; 1.3 percentage points above target; three consecutive months at or above 3.3%
Gas prices (national avg, Aug 6-7): $4.063/gallon down from $4.098 last week; 50% of US states now below $4.00; the oil price movements of this week have not yet fully transmitted to the pump; the net effect of this week's oil volatility ($81 to $75 to $77.75) will appear at pumps in mid-to-late August
Credit card APR (composite avg, Aug 2026): ~22.18% remains historically elevated; will not decline until the Fed cuts rates; the ADP miss and soft BLS risk have moved the rate-cut timeline earlier but not yet confirmed it; households carrying revolving balances are paying over $220 per year in interest for every $1,000 on their card
Personal savings rate (June): 2.7% spending continues to outrun income at the national level; at 44,000 new jobs per month, income growth decelerates further; if BLS confirms ADP this morning, the savings rate trajectory becomes even more concerning
Bottom Line
  • The Challenger data - 33,429 announced cuts in July, the lowest in two years, with AI as the top reason for the fifth month running - is the most granular description of what is actually happening to household employment. AI is eliminating specific roles (data processing, customer service, certain white-collar analytical tasks) at the same time that overall layoffs are historically low. The result is a labor market where the experience of job loss is concentrated in identifiable categories while the aggregate statistics remain benign. Claims at 199,000 are the aggregate statistic. The Challenger data is the category-level reality. For households in AI-exposed roles, the question of whether the labor market is "healthy" is not answered by the claims number.
  • Gas at $4.063 nationally - down from $4.098 last week - is the one unambiguous household budget positive of the week. The 2-to-4 week transmission lag means consumers are seeing crude from approximately two weeks ago at the pump, which was in the $79-82 range. If $77.75 WTI holds through next week, pump prices move toward $3.90-4.00 nationally by late August, potentially crossing below $4.00 for the first time since early July. That is approximately $15 per fill-up of relief on a 15-gallon tank compared to mid-July prices. For the median household spending $130 per month on fuel, that is roughly $30-40 per month in purchasing power recovery - real relief, but insufficient to offset a 2.7% savings rate and 22% credit card APR.

Crypto

Bitcoin held near $64,400 through Thursday and into Friday morning's pre-market as the labor market uncertainty and Hormuz deal ambiguity kept risk assets range-bound. The BLS report at 8:30 AM ET is the most direct near-term macro input for crypto as well as equities.

Bitcoin (BTC): ~$64,400 Thursday; ~$64,382 early Friday morning -0.69% Friday pre-market; holding above the $63,000 support level; institutional ETF inflows remain a structural bid - BlackRock's IBIT pulled in over $170 million of the $211 million in total daily ETF inflows Thursday; Bitcoin is not reacting materially to either the ADP miss or the claims strength, continuing its pattern of behavioral uncorrelation with both equity sectors
Ethereum (ETH): ~$1,907 Thursday neutral momentum; holding support at $1,807; the CLARITY Act continues to provide a constructive regulatory backdrop
XRP (Ripple): ~$1.06 Thursday close; ~$1.03 early Friday -3% across the two sessions; the $1.00 psychological level is the key near-term support; 52-week range $1.01-$3.38; two catalysts that had been priced in both failed to arrive - spot XRP ETF inflows collapsed to just $27.29 million for all of July (versus $666 million in the November 2025 launch month), and the CLARITY Act was sidelined when the Senate went on recess July 27 without a vote; a death cross has formed; ETF cumulative inflows remain positive at $1.44 billion since launch but momentum has stalled; classified as a Digital Commodity by the SEC and CFTC in March 2026 following the August 2025 settlement; on-chain metrics are weak - Daily Active Addresses at 25,350, second-lowest reading of 2026; Ripple's August 1 escrow unlock released 1 billion XRP but net new supply was only 300 million tokens
Rate-path context: September hike probability at approximately 40-50% heading into BLS; a soft BLS print (below 60,000) reduces September probability materially and is marginally positive for crypto via lower rate expectations; a strong BLS print (above 100,000) raises September probability and is modestly negative; neither outcome is large enough to drive Bitcoin outside its current $63,000-$66,000 range on its own
CLARITY Act context: crypto regulatory legislation advancing in Congress; providing a policy backdrop that has unlocked institutional allocators who required regulatory clarity before making material new allocations to digital assets
Bottom Line
  • Bitcoin's modest -0.69% Friday pre-market move, following a week that included a 28.7% crash in AppLovin, a 10%+ crash in SK Hynix, a 7% oil drop, and a 3.4% oil recovery, underscores the uncorrelation thesis that institutional Bitcoin buyers have been making for 18 months. The asset is simply not reacting to the week's volatility at scale. BlackRock's IBIT inflows at $170 million Thursday - the largest single-day ETF inflow this week - suggest that the institutional bid is treating Bitcoin's current $63,000-$66,000 range as an accumulation zone rather than a distribution zone. The BLS number at 8:30 AM ET will produce a short-term directional move in crypto as it produces moves across risk assets. But the structural story - regulatory clarity, institutional ETF flows, and behavioral uncorrelation from equity sectors - is unlikely to change on the basis of one employment report.
  • XRP at $1.03 reflects a stalled catalyst story. Two events that had been priced into XRP through much of 2026 - robust spot ETF inflows and the CLARITY Act's passage - have both lost momentum in the same month. ETF inflows fell to $27.29 million in July from a $666 million launch-month in November 2025; the Senate sidelined the CLARITY Act before its August recess without a vote. The result is a coin that was trading at $3.38 at its 52-week high and is now approaching the $1.00 floor - a 70% decline from peak. The regulatory structure is actually better than it was a year ago: the SEC settlement is complete, XRP is classified as a Digital Commodity, and the ETF infrastructure exists. But infrastructure without flows and legislation without passage produces the kind of chart XRP is drawing. Watch the $1.00 level and the Senate's return from recess.

Around the World

Asian markets edged modestly lower in the Friday session as semiconductor stocks stabilized after the week's severe declines. European markets closed positive Thursday, benefiting from declining crude ahead of the Dow's late-day sell-off. Brazil's ongoing rate-cut cycle continues to offer a potential preview of developed market rate trajectories.

Nikkei 225 (Thursday Aug 6): declined as semiconductor-related names remained under pressure; Tokyo Electron and Advantest extended losses from Wednesday as the Korean chip rout spread through the global semiconductor supply chain; broader TOPIX was more resilient, with non-tech names partially offsetting the chip-stock weakness
Korean market (context): the KOSPI's -4.58% decline on Wednesday (Samsung -6.3%, SK Hynix -10.37%) represented the most consequential single session in the Korean semiconductor market since July 31's historic 18% rebound; Goldman Sachs and JPMorgan maintained Buy ratings on SK Hynix and Samsung post-decline, noting forward P/E ratios of approximately 3.5-3.6x that are "detached from fundamentals" at current price levels; the circuit-breaker triggered briefly on the KOSPI during the session
Asian markets (Friday Asia session): edged modestly lower as markets awaited the BLS jobs report at 8:30 AM ET; the Middle East awaited a formal Hormuz announcement; semiconductor stocks stabilized after the week's severe declines rather than extending them
European markets (Thursday close): DAX +0.5%, FTSE 100 +0.4%, CAC 40 +0.1%, Euro Stoxx 50 +0.49% all closed positive before the US session deteriorated; European markets benefited from lower crude and solid corporate earnings; the positive European close reflects a continent whose manufacturing sector benefits directly from lower energy prices
Brazil: Copom delivered its fourth consecutive rate cut, lowering the Selic rate to 14.00%; Brazil's rate cycle is running approximately 12-18 months ahead of the US and other developed markets; in the scenario where Friday's BLS print is weak enough to put the first US rate cut on the table, Brazil's current cycle is the most relevant historical analogue for what that pivot looks like in practice
China Manufacturing PMI (July official): 49.2 first contraction in five months; weak domestic demand; the US-China manufacturing divergence - US ISM at 55.6%, China official PMI at 49.2% - continues to widen; AI infrastructure spending, semiconductor supply chains, and power infrastructure investment are concentrated outside China in this cycle
Bottom Line
  • The SK Hynix and Samsung declines from Wednesday are the international story that matters most for the AI investment thesis this week. Goldman Sachs and JPMorgan maintaining Buy ratings at 3.5-3.6x forward P/E for Samsung and SK Hynix is a signal that institutional money views Wednesday's decline as an inventory-cycle correction, not a demand-destruction event. The AI infrastructure spending cycle - Nvidia, AMD, hyperscaler capex - is not ending. The memory chip inventory build-up cycle that always accompanies rapid demand growth is producing the same kind of correction that semiconductor cycles always produce: supply catches up, inventory rises, prices and margins compress, stocks correct. What is happening in Korea is a mid-cycle inventory normalization within a multi-year structural demand expansion, not the end of the AI capex cycle. The distinction matters for anyone who owns semiconductor-adjacent assets.
  • European markets closing positive Thursday - DAX +0.5%, FTSE +0.4%, CAC +0.1% - reflect the most direct beneficiary of lower crude oil prices among the major developed market economies. Europe imports essentially all of its crude; a sustained WTI at $75-78 versus the mid-July peak near $93 is a direct GDP benefit equivalent to several tenths of a percentage point of growth. European manufacturing, which has been under pressure from Chinese competition and high energy costs, benefits disproportionately from lower oil. The DAX's positive close on a day when the Dow fell 464 points illustrates that energy-importing economies experience lower oil prices differently from oil-exporting or oil-neutral economies.
