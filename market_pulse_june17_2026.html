Weekly Briefing • Tuesday, June 17, 2026

Fed Holds Rates Steady in Warsh's First Meeting. Housing Starts Crash 15% to a Six-Year Low.

The FOMC held the federal funds rate at 3.50-3.75% as expected, but Chair Warsh's first press conference signaled a more hawkish tone than markets anticipated. Housing starts plunged 15.4% in May to 1.177 million - the lowest since May 2020. Retail sales surprised to the upside at +0.9%, though economists warn the tax-refund boost is fading. The Dow hit a fresh record at 52,003 while oil steadied near $76.

This Week in the News

The headlines shaping markets right now.

  • Fed holds rates at 3.50-3.75% - Warsh delivers first press conference as Chair: The FOMC voted unanimously to hold the federal funds rate at 3.50-3.75%, marking the fourth consecutive meeting without a change. Markets had priced in a 97% probability of a hold. The real event was Warsh's first press conference, where he acknowledged that inflation at 4.2% remains "uncomfortably above target" while noting that falling oil prices could change the calculus in the second half of the year.
  • Housing starts crash 15.4% - worst reading since May 2020: New residential construction plunged to a seasonally adjusted annual rate of 1.177 million in May. Multi-family starts collapsed 41.6% to 284,000 - the lowest since November 2024. Single-family starts slipped 1.9% to an eight-month low of 882,000. Builders are pulling back sharply as mortgage rates above 6.5% and rising inventory choke demand.
  • Retail sales beat expectations at +0.9% in May: U.S. retail and food services sales hit $763.7 billion, up 0.9% from April. Online sales rose 1.5%, clothing and furniture posted gains. But economists caution the strength came from tax refunds in April and May - a one-time boost that is already fading. Electronics and department stores declined.
  • Iran peace signing set for June 19 in Switzerland - markets are closed: The formal signing of the US-Iran peace deal falls on Juneteenth, when US equity markets are closed. The ceremony in Switzerland is expected to formally reopen the Strait of Hormuz and begin sanctions relief. Oil has already priced in the deal - WTI steady near $76, down from $91 less than two weeks ago. The risk is now in execution, not announcement.

The Quick Read

The Fed holding rates was expected, but the housing starts collapse was not. A 15.4% plunge in new construction to its lowest level since the pandemic shutdown is the clearest signal yet that high mortgage rates are breaking the housing supply pipeline. Multi-family starts falling 41.6% means the apartment boom that defined 2023-2024 is over. Meanwhile, retail sales beating expectations at +0.9% created a contradictory picture - the consumer is still spending, but on borrowed time as the tax-refund tailwind fades, the savings rate sits at 2.6%, and credit card delinquencies approach 2008 levels. Gold surged past $4,350, foreclosure filings rose 13% year-over-year, and the Dow hit a record at 52,003.

The FOMC decision itself was a non-event - the fourth straight hold at 3.50-3.75%. What mattered was Warsh's tone. By acknowledging inflation at 4.2% as "uncomfortably above target" without offering a clear timeline for cuts, he gave the bond market nothing to reprice. The 10-year held at 4.44%, the 2-year at 4.06%, and the 30-year Treasury stayed just below 5%. Mortgages at 6.52% are unchanged. The housing market needed a dovish signal to break out of its freeze. It did not get one.

Retail sales at +0.9% look strong on the surface, but the composition tells a different story. Online sales rose 1.5% and clothing stores posted gains, but electronics and department stores declined. The Census Bureau noted that generous tax refunds in April and May boosted spending. Strip out that one-time effect and the consumer is treading water. With the personal savings rate at 2.6% - the lowest since 2008 - and 47% of BNPL users paying late, the consumer resilience narrative has an expiration date. The question is whether it runs out before or after the Fed cuts.

The housing starts collapse deserves its own read. Single-family starts falling to an eight-month low while multi-family starts hit their lowest since November 2024 means builders are responding to the demand freeze in real time. But fewer starts today means less inventory 12-18 months from now. The housing market is caught in a vicious cycle: rates too high to build, but not building makes the eventual shortage worse. If rates do not come down by fall, the 2027 supply pipeline is already damaged.

Stock Market

Dow hits a record. Nasdaq dips on FOMC caution. Russell 2000 pulls back as rate-cut hopes fade.

S&P 500: 5,518 +0.1% day
Dow Jones: 52,003 +0.6% day (new record)
Nasdaq: 26,466 +0.3% day
Russell 2000 (small caps): 2,913 −0.9% day
Fear gauge (VIX): 16.41 steady
Bottom Line
  • The Dow hitting a record at 52,003 while the Russell 2000 dropped 0.9% is a classic divergence that shows large-cap safety trades outperforming rate-sensitive small caps. The FOMC's refusal to signal near-term cuts punished the very stocks that rallied last week on rate-cut optimism. If the dot plot did not move, the Russell gives back more.
  • The VIX holding at 16.41 - essentially flat - on a day the Fed announced its rate decision is unusual. Markets are not celebrating and not panicking. They are waiting. The Iran signing on June 19 (with markets closed for Juneteenth) means the next trading day reaction comes Monday, June 23. That gap creates an information vacuum where volatility can build.
  • The S&P barely budging at +0.1% tells you the market got exactly what it expected from the FOMC - no surprise in either direction. The next catalyst is the PCE inflation report on June 26, the Fed's preferred measure. If PCE shows deceleration from the oil crash, the rate-cut narrative revives. If it stays hot, the "higher for longer" grind continues.

Interest Rates

Fed holds steady. Yields barely moved. Warsh's first press conference offered no surprises - and that was the surprise.

Fed policy rate: 3.50-3.75% (held, 4th consecutive meeting)
2-year Treasury: 4.06% steady
10-year Treasury: 4.44% −4 bps from last wk
30-year Treasury: 4.94% −3 bps from last wk
30-year mortgage (avg.): 6.52% −6 bps from last wk
Bottom Line
  • The 30-year Treasury dropping below 5% to 4.94% - and staying there through the FOMC - is the bond market saying it believes inflation has peaked even if the Fed has not confirmed it. The long end is pricing in the oil crash. If WTI holds below $80 through the Iran signing, the 30-year could test 4.85% by month end.
  • Mortgages at 6.52% are down from 6.58% last week but still far above the level needed to unfreeze the housing market. The housing starts collapse at -15.4% is what rates above 6.50% produce. Builders need rates below 6.00% to justify new construction at scale. That requires two Fed cuts at minimum - which the dot plot is not yet projecting for 2026.
  • The 2-year at 4.06% and the 10-year at 4.44% give a spread of 38 basis points - a normally sloped curve. The curve has been telling you for weeks that the economy is functioning, not in crisis, but also not growing fast enough to justify current rate levels. The Fed agrees, which is why they held. But holding is not a policy - it is a placeholder.

At $50,000 deposited, that gap costs roughly $1,150 per year in foregone interest. Moving to an online bank takes 15 minutes.

Oil steadying after its freefall. Gas prices finally following crude lower. The Iran signing on Thursday is the next catalyst.

Brent crude: $79.45/bbl −2.2% from last wk
National gas avg: $4.03/gal −$0.14 from last wk
West Coast: $5.36-5.70/gal (CA highest at $5.70)
Cheapest region (South Central): $3.39/gal (Indiana)
Bottom Line
  • Gas prices finally broke below $4.03 nationally - down from $4.55 at the May peak. That is an 11% decline in under a month, and it is the number most Americans notice. If oil holds below $80 through the Iran signing on Thursday, gas prices continue falling toward $3.75-3.85 by early July. Every 10-cent drop in gas is roughly $14 billion in annualized consumer savings.
  • WTI steadying around $76 after crashing from $91 is the market waiting for the ink to dry. The deal is "complete" but not signed. Thursday's ceremony in Switzerland will either confirm the price floor at $72-76 or, if anything disrupts the signing, snap oil back above $85. Markets are closed for Juneteenth on Thursday, so any disruption trades out Monday - with a full weekend of positioning built in.
  • The $2.31 spread between Indiana ($3.39) and California ($5.70) continues to widen as state-level taxes and regulations dominate the West Coast price structure. California drivers are paying 68% more than the cheapest states for the same gallon of gas. The oil crash benefits them least because state taxes and refinery costs are fixed.

Commodities

Gold surges past $4,350 on FOMC day. Silver near $70. Copper holds record highs. Cattle pulling back from $255.

Gold / oz: $4,354 +4.8% wk
Silver / oz: $70.42 +4.1% wk
Copper / lb: $6.51 +0.3% day, near record
Corn / bushel: $4.13 −0.7% wk
Soybeans / bushel: $11.38 +1.7% wk
Live cattle / cwt: $236.15 −7.4% from $255 record
Bottom Line
  • Gold surging to $4,354 on FOMC day - up nearly 5% on the week - is the market's vote of no confidence in the Fed's ability to control inflation. If the Fed were seen as credibly hawkish, gold would be falling. Instead, gold is at record highs because the market reads "hold" as "the Fed is stuck" - too afraid to hike into a housing crash, too stubborn to cut into 4.2% CPI. Gold above $4,300 is an inflation hedge, not a trade.
  • Copper holding near $6.51 - record territory - while oil crashes is the divergence that matters for the real economy. Oil is falling on geopolitical supply relief. Copper is rising on structural demand from AI data centers, the energy transition, and global electrification. One commodity says inflation is easing; the other says the underlying demand cycle is not slowing.
  • Cattle pulling back to $236.15 from the $255 record is healthy profit-taking. But the structural shortage remains - the US herd at 86.2 million head is its smallest since 1951. Every pullback in cattle is temporary until the herd rebuilds, which takes 3-4 years minimum. Beef prices will be elevated through 2028 at least.

Crop Weather

Drought still covering 56% of the Lower 48. Kansas wheat harvest accelerating under stress. El Nino probability at 87-92%.

Corn: 67% good-to-excellent. Planting complete. Illinois remains the risk zone - 42% abnormally dry, 20% in moderate drought. Critical pollination window approaching in July.
Winter wheat: Kansas 15% good-to-excellent. Oklahoma 13%. Harvest 23% complete in Oklahoma. Production forecast: 1,048M bushels - lowest since 1965.
Drought: 56% of the Lower 48. 75% of US counties affected. Record-low snowpack in 8 western states depleting reservoirs heading into summer.
El Nino watch: 87-92% probability through January 2027. Historically dries the Midwest during growing season - worst-case scenario for corn pollination.
Bottom Line
  • The wheat situation is locked in as a disaster. Kansas at 15% good-to-excellent and the production forecast at its worst since 1965 cannot be reversed by rain at this point. Harvest is underway and the damage is done. Higher flour, bread, and cereal prices in Q3 and Q4 are a certainty - the only question is magnitude. Food inflation runs on its own clock regardless of what happens with oil or the Fed.
  • Corn at 67% good-to-excellent looks adequate now, but July is the month that makes or breaks the crop. The Illinois drought at 42% abnormally dry during the approach to pollination is the setup for rapid deterioration if moisture does not arrive. With El Nino at 87-92% probability bringing historically drier Midwest conditions, the risk is skewed to the downside for corn yields.
  • Drought covering 56% of the Lower 48 with 75% of counties affected and western reservoirs at record lows is a multi-year infrastructure problem. Even normal rainfall this summer does not restore reservoir levels. Water restrictions, agricultural stress, and fire risk are structural through 2027 at minimum.

Real Estate

Housing starts crash to a six-year low. Foreclosures keep climbing. The split market is breaking.

Housing starts (May): 1.177M SAAR −15.4% MoM (lowest since May 2020)
Multi-family starts: 284,000 −41.6% MoM
Single-family starts: 882,000 −1.9% MoM (8-month low)
Foreclosure filings May: 40,355 properties +13% YoY
Foreclosure starts May: 27,304 +13% YoY
Bank repossessions / REOs May: 4,092 +6% YoY
Mortgage delinquency rate: 4.44% +40 bps YoY
FHA delinquency rate: ~11.5% highest since Q2 2021
Existing-home sales (annual pace): 4.17M
% listings cutting price: 36% nationally; up to 44% in pandemic boom markets
Inventory: ~4.5 months of supply (highest since 2020)
30-year mortgage: 6.52%
Bottom Line
  • Housing starts crashing 15.4% to their lowest since May 2020 is the most important data point of the day. Multi-family starts collapsing 41.6% to 284,000 means the apartment construction pipeline is shutting down. This is builders voting with their balance sheets that demand at current rates does not justify construction. Fewer starts now means a supply shortage in 2027-2028 that makes affordability worse, not better.
  • Foreclosure filings rising 13% year-over-year to 40,355 in May - the ninth straight month of YoY increases - confirms the distress trend is accelerating. FHA delinquencies at 11.5%, their highest since Q2 2021, show the stress is concentrated in the borrowers who could least afford it. These are first-time buyers with low down payments who bought near peak prices with high rates.
  • The FOMC holding rates today means no relief is coming for housing. Builders needed a dovish signal to justify restarting projects. They did not get it. Homebuyers needed mortgage rates to break below 6.25% to make the math work. They will not see that until the Fed cuts at least twice. The housing freeze continues through the summer at minimum.
  • Inventory at 4.5 months of supply looks like normalization, but the composition is distorted. A growing share is distressed (REOs up 6%, foreclosure starts up 13%). Another share is price cuts (36% nationally, 44% in pandemic boomtowns). Healthy inventory growth comes from confident sellers listing voluntarily. This inventory growth comes from sellers who have no choice.

Household Finance

Retail sales beat but the consumer's balance sheet tells a different story. Delinquencies at or near records across every credit category.

Retail sales (May): $763.7B +0.9% MoM
Total household debt: $18.8 trillion record
Credit card debt: ~$1.18 trillion; avg APR 21.5%
Credit card delinquency: approaching 2008 crisis levels (NY Fed)
Avg. new-car payment: $748/mo subprime: $792/mo
Auto loan 60+ days delinquent: 1.54% record high (NY Fed)
BNPL users paying late: 47% +6 pts YoY
Personal savings rate: 2.6% (lowest since 2008)
Bottom Line
  • Retail sales at +0.9% and consumer balance sheets at 2008-era stress levels are not contradictory - they are sequential. The consumer is spending because they have to, not because they can afford to. The tax-refund boost that drove May sales is a one-time event. When it fades, the 2.6% savings rate and record delinquencies become the dominant story. The spending is the last chapter before the pullback, not a sign of health.
  • The FOMC holding rates at 3.50-3.75% means credit card APRs stay at 21.5%, auto loan rates stay elevated, and the consumer squeeze continues. Every month rates stay here, the delinquency pipeline grows. Auto loans at a record 1.54% 60-day delinquency rate, credit cards approaching 2008 levels, and 47% of BNPL users paying late are three independent measures all saying the same thing: the consumer is maxed out.
  • The most telling stat remains the personal savings rate at 2.6% - the lowest since 2008. This means any unexpected expense (medical bill, car repair, job loss) becomes a debt event immediately. There is no buffer. The consumer is one disruption away from a credit contraction, and the FOMC just told them rates are not coming down anytime soon.

Bottom Line
  • Bitcoin holding above $65,000 after a 5.7% weekly gain but fading on FOMC day is the pattern repeating. Crypto rallies on hope (Iran deal, rate-cut expectations) and stalls on reality (no cuts, Warsh noncommittal). BTC is still down 49% from its $128,198 all-time high in October 2025. The weekly bounce is trading, not conviction. A sustained move above $70,000 requires either a Fed cut or a major new catalyst.
  • XRP dropping 8.1% on the week while Bitcoin and Ethereum rallied is notable. XRP ETF inflows have not been enough to offset broader selling pressure. The divergence between BTC/ETH (up) and XRP (down) suggests money is concentrating into the two largest assets and abandoning speculative positions as rate uncertainty persists.
  • Solana ETF assets crossing $1 billion is a structural milestone even if the price at $72.41 is subdued. Institutional infrastructure for SOL is expanding. But the price remains down 63% from its 2024 highs, and the gap between institutional adoption (growing) and price performance (flat) is a tension that resolves one way or the other by Q4.

Around the World

Japan hits new records on export surge. Europe benefits from oil crash. China treads water. Emerging markets wait on tariff expiration.

Japan: Nikkei at 69,902 +0.7% day. Fresh record high. May exports jumped 17% YoY - the fastest pace since November 2022. The BOJ rate hike to 1.0% has not slowed the rally. Weak yen and AI investment continue to drive the world's best-performing major index in 2026.
Europe: Stoxx 600 +1.7% wk. Benefiting from oil crash more than the US - European industry is more energy-dependent. ECB at 2.25%. Italy's FTSE MIB led with +3.2%. France's CAC 40 +1.6%.
China: Shanghai Composite holding above 4,000 on government floor buying. May retail sales fell 0.6% YoY - the first consumer spending decline since Covid reopening. Fixed-asset investment down 4.1%. Property slump now in its fourth year. The market is sustained by policy while fundamentals deteriorate.
Emerging: MSCI EM under pressure from tariff proposals on 60 economies and strong dollar. July 24 temporary tariff expiration is the next binary event. If tariffs become permanent, EM capital flight accelerates.
Bottom Line
  • Japan's Nikkei at 69,902 - another record - on the back of a 17% YoY export surge is the most impressive macro story in global markets. Japan is benefiting from the weak yen making exports cheap, the AI boom driving semiconductor demand, and the BOJ's confidence signal in hiking to 1.0%. The Nikkei is up roughly 30% year-to-date - far outpacing every other major index globally.
  • Europe benefits disproportionately from the oil crash because European industry is more energy-dependent than the US. The Stoxx 600's 1.7% weekly gain reflects improved margins as input costs fall. If Iran signs on Thursday and oil stays below $80, European equities have further upside even without ECB cuts. The energy dividend flows directly to corporate earnings.
  • The July 24 tariff expiration is becoming the next major global risk event. If temporary tariffs on 60 economies become permanent, supply chains reroute, EM currencies weaken, and the dollar strengthens further. If they expire or get reduced, it reverses the capital flows that have punished emerging markets all year. Five weeks is not far away.
